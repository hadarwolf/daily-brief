I was reading a book and stumbled onto a claim that there was an experiment. A group of students learning pottery was divided into two groups. One group was told that they would be evaluated based on volume, i.e. the number of pieces they produced. The other group was told that they would be evaluated based on the quality of their best piece. Then both groups were compared based on the quality of the best piece, and the "quantity" group showed better results.

I was extremely intrigued by this experiment and was curious to understand how big the groups were and how they evaluated the quality of a pottery piece (there must be some way to quantify that for the sake of the comparison?).

To my surprise, this wasn't a scientific study, but an anecdote from the book Art & Fear: Observations on the Perils (and Rewards) of Artmaking by David Bayles and Ted Orland (page 29 of the Image Continuum edition).[1]So all the details available about this result are:

This is already disappointing, since this is basically an anecdote about a study, and not a very well set up one. However, it gets worse. In 2016 both coauthors separately admitted that they got the story from someone else, and that the original story had nothing to do with ceramics: it was about photography. David Bayles told a Hacker News reader by email that it was "a university level photography class taught by 'a very experienced and sophisticated teacher'" and that apart from the medium it was "the literal truth".[2]Ted Orland told James Clear, in an email dated October 18, 2016, that the book "faithfully captures the scene as Jerry told it to me—except I replaced photography with ceramics as the medium being explored".[3]Jerry is the photographer Jerry Uelsmann, who taught at the University of Florida and was Orland's collaborator for decades.[4]

Why did they change the subject? Both authors are photographers. Orland says they were "consciously trying to broaden the range of media being referenced in the text", and admits "it would've been easier to retain photography".[3]Bayles put it more bluntly to his correspondent: they already had too many photography examples.[2]

The photo detail changes the nature of the experiment. It is fairly easy and cheap to take a photo compared to making a pottery piece. So one way to explain why the quantity group produced better results is to just say that each shot had a (not perfectly correlated) probability of being the best, thus more shots = more lottery tickets.

There's a model for why it makes sense to take many shots that I like which is asking to what extent having a shot at goal will teach you about how to improve. It is about the extent to which it is skill vs luck where skill is defined as the mean|action counterfactual influence is high whilst in luck domains it is not.

How do you know whether it is a skill based domain or not? Well you have to at least start trying first! In order to lower your variance you need some degree of dataset first so the strategy is most likely to take more shots in the beginning and over time as you start recognizing the skills you might want to start polishing subareas of it.

I'm gonna copy paste my comment from this shortform as it essentially talks a bit about how you can model this:

There's a nice book called the success equation that makes a point similar to the following paper about skill vs luck based domains where a skill domain is something where you're able to get repeat successes without much variance and a luck domain where things are more random. There's a sort of implicit model in the photography thing where you have higher variance.

The advice is to take the most amount of shots in the luck based domain as that is all that matters there but what about the skill based domain?

Well, there you want to (approximately) maximise your learning so we might say that the actions we should pick should be the ones that are dependent on us learning something. A naive approach is something like max(n*p) where p is the probability of learning and n is the amount of opportunities you have to learn. Sometimes you don't want to do this because the skill lies in highly specific skills which means you want to spend a longer time on one attempt so that you get this really right. This then essentially boils down to a learning problem where priors are inherited through previous people often (e.g different sports have different training regimes).

But what do you do if you don't know the extent to which something is luck vs skill?

I think the answer basically becomes to just do the photography thing again and see what the variance becomes over time, treat your unknown domain is as if it were a skill domain and see if it lowers the variance of it. If it does it probably is, if it isn't then you've already done the strategy of taking many shots which means you've done the right thing.

I have tried this out in practice over the last year or so and I've concluded that mentorship is probably a more skill based domain than I originally thought and that writing is more luck based than I thought (I might just not have enough data on this one though.)

Clarification after chatting with claude: It's the conditional probability of the mean moving that matters p(mean|action). Secondly it is about feedback signal clarity for learning, can you move fast and get a clean signal? (e.g blitz chess) Or does moving fast make it so that you can't learn (e.g trying to learn to code by using claude to vibe-code apps that you don't even look at the code of).

There's also some annoying stuff around how getting high variance in the results doesn't mean the domain isn't skill based since it is only the conditional on the mean moving that matters. So the writing thing might actually not be true in terms of being a non-skilled domain, it is more that it is inherently high variance in the feedback domain.
