We offer a concrete proposal for pacing the frontier.

1. Transparency: Verified Compute Allocation Disclosures (VCAD)

2. Reallocation: Shift compute away from capabilities R&D

The first component is Verified Compute Allocation Disclosures (VCAD): AI companies regularly disclose their compute usage, and an independent auditor verifies the disclosures. The second component is to reallocate compute away from capabilities R&D and toward other use cases, such as serving customers and safety research (non-capabilities R&D).

We roughly estimate that pacing via compute reallocation starting January 2027, with a 15% cap, would delay the intelligence explosion by a year. This is most likely insufficient to prevent AI takeover (or mitigate other problems created by RSI), and so this year should be used to implement a more durable policy regime such as Plan A or Plan S.

Nearly every approach to pacing, including ones built on safety and security requirements, strongly benefits from VCAD:

With VCAD, competitors and the public can trust a company that says it is pacing. As evidence of risk grows, this could enable a “race to the top”. If regulation later mandates pacing, a VCAD process that companies have already built and stress-tested makes that regulation faster to write and more workable.

Given the urgency of the situation, we elected to publish this relatively quickly. We are not very confident in any given detail of our proposal. Nevertheless, we think it represents a step forward in concreteness for pacing proposals and hope that we and others can quickly turn this into something workable.

If all we want is transparency, we think that baseline auditing would most likely be sufficient. However, if there is also a limit set on capabilities R&D compute — as we think there should be — then the stricter the limit, the greater the incentive to cheat. Baseline auditing would be insufficient to reliably verify pacing.

We think that if frontier AI companies were legally required to (i) disclose how they use their compute and (ii) use no more than X% on capabilities R&D, then the most likely outcome would be that all five leading U.S. companies comply. This is for the same reasons that they normally comply with laws — too many possible whistleblowers or other ways they could get caught. This is especially true since the point of noncompliance would be to get an advantage in AI R&D progress, but that advantage would be hard to hide — if your AI models leapfrog over those of your competitors, for example, that would raise suspicions. That being said, we think that cheating is a serious possibility, especially the kind where you do it in a plausibly deniable way so that if you get caught you don’t go straight to jail.

There are several ways our proposal could be implemented and evolve over time. Some of the ways it can vary include the number of companies it applies to, whether it is voluntary or required by regulation, the level of granularity in disclosures, and the level of verification in place.

The pilot we propose is voluntary disclosures with third-party auditing. AI companies voluntarily disclose their compute allocations per our proposal and allow auditors to immediately carry out baseline auditing and then work up to robust auditing.

AI companies are in a collective action problem: they are proceeding faster than they believe optimal. VCAD supplies two elements that can help resolve the problem, prompting a race to the top.

Interest in pacing is high and will grow with evidence of risk. Demand for reduced R&D allocations gives companies a self-interested reason to reallocate and rewards those that do, spurring a race to the top. Each reallocation would be a unilateral response to, among other things, public demand based on a public benchmark. The dynamic does not depend on, and should not involve, agreement among companies on allocation levels.

To be effective, disclosures must be trusted within a reasonable margin of error (e.g., <5% of total compute). The burden of verification will increase with how strong the incentives are to misreport and which actors are involved.

This depends on what the capabilities R&D dial is set to, how much error there is in the regime, and what the production function for AI progress is with respect to R&D compute. If we assume the capabilities allocation is set to 20% and 6% error is exploited, then the resulting 26% allocation would be an immediate ~3x reduction in capabilities R&D compute for Anthropic (which uses an estimated ~75% of its compute on capabilities R&D today).

We don’t know how much overall AI progress would slow down due to an ongoing 3x reduction in R&D compute, because there are other inputs to AI progress and we don’t know how important each of them is. However, our best guess is that it would slow progress down by around 1.5-2x[3] in the short term. Over time, the pace of progress is determined by the growth rate of compute, so assuming the company’s compute buildout is similar under pacing, its speed would converge back to the previous rate.

Imagine a phantom copy of the company that has 3x less compute, growing at the same rate. This phantom company would follow behind the leader with a fixed calendar time delay, but move at the same pace (because it has as much compute as the frontier company had a fixed time ago).[4] The one-time 3x reduction in compute puts the frontier company in the phantom company’s position from that point onward, so its progress converges towards the phantom company’s trajectory.

As a result, later capability milestones are delayed more (the delay is still growing towards the fixed calendar lag), but the slowdown in the rate at which milestones are crossed shrinks towards zero. So the compute reduction mainly pushes back timelines, and its effect on the speed of takeoff is smaller the further away takeoff is. This assumes compute growth is constant; if compute growth slows in the future (as is likely, absent large AI-driven speedups), then takeoff could also be slower. In practice, though, timelines to highly capable AI might be short enough that this convergence doesn’t have time to matter much.

In a run of our AI Futures Model (which, without pacing, reaches Superhuman AI Researcher (SAR) in August 2027 and Top-Expert-Dominating-AI (TED-AI) in October 2027), a 3x cut from January 2027 delays SAR by about 4 months and TED-AI by about 5 months, and the paced company crosses both about 24% slower than it would without pacing.[5][6]

We can think of the options for slowing down AI progress by looking at the set of inputs to AI progress, which are R&D compute and labor, where R&D compute can be used on experiments, training runs and AI automation.

Each of these inputs could be limited in quantity (e.g., a cap on compute spend) or regulated by safety standards (e.g., banning training runs that use neuralese algorithms). The decision to restrict inputs or require safeguards could be part of a pacing regime that is triggered by reaching a pre-registered risk level (e.g., safety-case-based pacing), reaching a pre-registered capability level (e.g., RSPs), or one that has no pre-registered triggers.

Writing good safety standards that sufficiently address the sources of risk will require a lot of institutional capacity, increased scientific understanding, and ongoing iteration, so this approach cannot be deployed quickly as a pacing measure.

Therefore, limiting inputs to AI progress is the more promising near-term pacing solution. However, limits that directly gate AI capabilities (without also gating algorithmic progress) could create an algorithmic overhang.

If we slow down via anything but limiting algorithmic progress, this creates an algorithmic overhang, which, once training resumes, results in a rapid return to something similar to the original trend (though it’s somewhat lower due to reduced AI R&D uplift). This algorithmic overhang could, in principle, become very large. Algorithmic progress is fast and is likely to accelerate in the future: estimates of algorithmic progress today range from 3x to 30x per year, and it seems plausible that algorithmic progress recently has been even faster. Because algorithmic progress is more rapid than compute progress (which is ~5x/yr), and all of the built-up progress could be immediately applied to a new training run while applying a compute overhang would be intermediated by R&D, an algorithmic overhang could be much worse than a compute overhang.
