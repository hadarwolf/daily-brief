A very simple point that I've had to explain to many people, so I thought I'd make a post on it.

Some classes of people in the past were economically necessary for their labor, but militarily irrelevant and coercible: slaves, serfs. Others didn't do any economic work, but were militarily important: the aristocracy! Living fun lives, partying, traveling, making and enjoying art. Some of them felt a bit of ennui due to not working, but mostly it wasn't a big problem.

The reason aristocracy gave way to democracy wasn't because of rising productivity of the masses: a slave in a diamond mine can be very productive, but still remain a slave. Rather, it was because firearms lowered the cost of training a soldier, and thus made the masses valuable as potential soldiers. That gave the masses a bargaining chip which led to better economic and political standing.

If Alice wishes for a world where her labor (e.g. as a programmer) remains economically valuable, but all military jobs go to AI, then humans will lose the "potential soldier bonus" we've had for a couple centuries and revert to a powerless, exploited position. The economic importance of our labor won't save us.

Whereas if Bob wishes for a world where all jobs in the economy are done by AI, but humans remain militarily important, then humans will be the aristocracy in that world and lead lives of leisure and fun. If I could magically make that future happen, I would.

Of course it's all academic: all military jobs can probably be done by AI even sooner than other jobs. There's already surveillance and drones. But still I wanted to write it up, because so many people today think economic irrelevance is the main issue, when it isn't.

I think this is directionally correct, but overstated. Sure, in the extreme case where group A has perfect military power, they can use that to extract ~all wealth from group B, even if group B otherwise would have had economic relevance. We might wind up in this situation as a result of improvements in AI, although I think it's more likely that unfriendly superintelligence doesn't need civilian or military labor from humans.In practice, in human history so far, all military power has been less-than-absolute, which means that if group B has significant economic power but no military power, then group B still has some leverage, because attempting to coerce group B to serve you by threats of violence is at least a partially negative-sum transaction. Group B won't work quite as hard or as skillfully under military occupation as they would if they were instead voluntarily trading with you. This is part of why, e.g., workers today in the United Kingdom still earn above subsistence wages even though they mostly don't carry firearms and mostly aren't allowed to. It's also a major theme of, e.g., the Cold War. West Germans had ~no military ability to resist NATO, and East Germans had ~no military ability to resist the Soviet Union; after all, both halves of Germany had just been thoroughly defeated in World War II. Nevertheless, German workers on both sides of the Iron Curtain were paid significantly more than subsistence wages because the superpowers wanted (some of) them to have more motivation to deploy their economically valuable skills. The Soviets could and did use violence to suppress direct attacks on their rule, but they mostly kept the more advanced German factories operating by paying the Germans with commodities that the Germans wanted, rather than by sending troops to watch over the German factory workers with loaded weapons.

Maybe the point I wanted to make (about military relevance of humans) got rounded off a bit to a different point (about stronger groups dominating weaker ones). I'm not saying that the stronger group will pay the weaker group subsistence wages! I'm saying that if members of the weaker group are potentially useful as soldiers, that in itself will lead to them being paid more and allowed to participate in politics. And if they can't be of use as soldiers, even when they're super useful economically, they'll be pushed down to subsistence and exploitation.

This potential-usefulness-as-soldiers is a fragile, historically unusual thing. When weapons cost much more than a person, a small, well trained weapon-owning class ruled the world, and everyone else suffered what they must. Then firearms appeared and gave us the current brief window of democracy and sharing of spoils. When military robots don't require human operators and become cheaper than a person for the same amount of military efficiency, the window will be over.

This is a timely post. I think the gradual disempowerment thesis over-emphasised economic value as THE way citizens resist being oppressed by governments. Military relevance is definitely another aspect of citizens' bargaining power (and this one has been reasonably well studied by historians over the years — just ask any AI to summarise the research, it goes all the way back to Aristotle.). But I do feel there's a more general analysis worth doing (I've been trying to do this myself this year) into how groups attain and use leverage of various kinds — military power, economic contributions, information asymmetries, ability to coordinate among themselves, etc — depending on the time and place.

Indeed, research into the democratic age (roughly 1750-now) tends to emphasise neither military nor ecomomic factors, but the ability of large enough portions of the citizenry to pose a credible threat (in the form of sabotage, strike, rebellion, etc.) as the bedrock they can use to secure further leverage the government via laws and voting.

This is an extremely important topic imo but it's really only tackled piecemeal by a few economic historians, a few anthropologists, some political scientists. Some good sources: Acemoglu & Robinson (2006), Economic Origins of Dictatorship and Democracy; Dahl (1989), Democracy and Its Critics; Pettit (2012), On the People’s Terms: A Republican Theory and Model of Democracy).
