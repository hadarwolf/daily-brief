I will repeat my general claim that the disempowerment critiques of strong AI are long on emotion and short on social science, marginal thinking, and comparative analysis. The beast that disempowered me from writing all of this blog post myself seems to agree. And that same beast offers some very nice general remarks:

I would particularly like to see a treatment of the general-equilibrium effects of AI on the power of humans over other humans. Much of what currently limits human agency is not the absence of cognitive abilities per se, but dependence on other people who possess scarce cognitive abilities.

Furthermore, many disempowerment scenarios seem to assume that AI capabilities will continue improving while the technologies available to humans for controlling, monitoring, bargaining with, and directing those AIs remain comparatively static. Why should that be the appropriate default assumption?

The strongest counterargument is that empowerment might be temporary and revocable, whereas eventual disempowerment might be irreversible. That asymmetry deserves serious consideration, but it too requires explicit modeling rather than simply counting the decisions delegated to machines.

And do keep in mind the comparative analysis. The same assumptions that get you disempowerment from AI (e.g., mental laziness) also tend to get you disempowerment from growing wealth, albeit at a likely slower pace. The marginal increase in disempowerment, should the risks of AI lead us to fall into that path, may not be so great in the longer term? Maybe so, but that is not a question you see being raised very often.
