At its core, every cell is just a bubble of chemistry sealed inside a membrane. In modern cells, that chemistry is extraordinarily complex, orchestrating some 1 billion reactions per second. Likewise, its container, the lipid membrane, is built and maintained by elaborate biochemical mechanisms. But even the much simpler first cells would have needed a bubble too.

Without a physical boundary corralling molecules into a small space, whatever it was that turned into early life would have been too dispersed to assemble the complex systems that life requires, said Bruce Damer, an astrobiologist at the University of California, Santa Cruz. “You need a container before you can get the chemistry.”

To some researchers, the cell membrane seems so essential to life’s functions that they propose it as the origin of life. In this framework, known as the “lipid first” hypothesis, Earth transitioned from a mess of primordial chemistry to something recognizably alive because of those containers.

But many proponents of the lipid world go even further. They argue that the bubbles did more than hold chemistry. The bubbles themselves began to evolve.

Evolution requires a way for parents to pass traits to offspring. Modern life accomplishes this with complex biochemicals such as DNA and RNA, which can encode and copy information in their sequences. In the 1990s, researchers imagined a different possibility: What if a chemical system, rather than a molecule, could carry heritable information and become subject to evolution?

The idea begins with a puddle of water on the early Earth. The puddle is stocked with all kinds of small organic molecules, some homegrown and some delivered by dust and meteorites. Many of these molecules have a split personality. One end of them is drawn to water, or “hydrophilic.” The other is repelled by water in the same way that oil is. These “hydrophobic” ends will huddle together to hide from the water, while the hydrophilic ends will face outward. These behaviors cause the molecules to form a tiny sphere — a bubble.

The primordial puddle also has other molecules, simpler raw ingredients that can, under the correct circumstances, be turned into more of themselves. Here’s how that works: Imagine that a bubble contains two special molecules, molecule A and molecule B. Each of these molecules speeds up a chemical reaction that uses other ingredients in the puddle. Molecule A, in this case, speeds up chemical reactions that produce molecule B. After a while, the bubble fills up with B’s. These, in turn, may speed up the production of another kind of molecule.

Sometimes, there might be a puddle where B’s return the favor and make more A’s. The A’s make more B’s, then the B’s make more A’s. Each type keeps the other topped up in an endless loop. The bubble containing these A’s and B’s will grow, but the ratio of molecules will stay more or less steady.

In time, the bubble will split, and its molecules will be dealt out into its baby bubbles. Each of these may get some A’s and some B’s, and the symbiotic loop will keep running, building the parent’s compositional recipe more faithfully than chance alone would. Unlike with DNA, which is copied exactly, letter by letter, a proportion of molecules gets passed down to the next generation of bubbles.

But evolution needs more than heredity. It needs to allow for change. In our chemical soup, that possibility comes from a stray molecule C, which like a mutation tips a bubble into a different molecular loop. Faster-growing bubbles split more often and crowd out slower-growing bubbles, so the fast-moving descendants will make up a bigger and bigger share of the crowd. Slow recipes get squeezed out.

In 2000, Doron Lancet, a geneticist at the Weizmann Institute of Science, and Daniel Segrè, a computational biologist at Boston University, published the results of a computer model that explored whether these dynamics could lead to lifelike behavior. They found that bubbles can pass their compositions to their daughters, which vary and compete, fulfilling the requirements for natural selection. In their computer model, at least, the lipid-world idea passed its first test.

Which molecules could build these bubbles? In the original simulation, molecules A, B, and the rest would be different kinds of lipids. The team settled on lipids, the molecules that make up modern cell membranes, in part because they mirror the model’s required half-hydrophobic, half-hydrophilic nature. When lipids are dropped into water, they can assemble into double-layered sheets. Under the right conditions, the sheets close in on themselves, forming a sphere with a pocket of water trapped inside.

But in the 25 years since Lancet, Segrè, and their colleagues published the original lipid-world hypothesis, scientists have questioned whether the first compartments had to be made of lipids at all. Couldn’t other types of molecules have formed a container first? “There really isn’t a distinct lipid-world hypothesis anymore,” Damer said. Researchers have proposed that certain minerals, such as iron sulfides, could have formed the walls of a container, or that coacervates — liquidlike droplets where molecules gather and concentrate — could have acted as membrane-free vessels for early chemistry.

Damer and David Deamer, a Santa Cruz biochemist who worked with Lancet and Segrè on the original hypothesis, have stayed focused on lipids, but in a specific setting: hot-spring pools. They now study whether the repeated drying and refilling of such pools could have squeezed simple molecules between layers of lipids to develop increasingly complex systems. Damer views their more recent “hot spring hypothesis” as an extension of the lipid world.

Origins-of-life research is famously contentious, and scientists in the field debate the lipid world against other ideas about how life formed. “The idea of a lipid-only form of primitive life has never made sense to me,” said Jack Szostak, a Nobel Prize–winning biochemist at the University of Chicago. He cited the lack of experimental evidence that a lipid-based system can evolve. Plus, he said, how even simple lipids would have formed on the early Earth is “the least well-developed aspect of all of prebiotic chemistry.”

Another criticism strikes at the heart of the original computer simulation. A 2010 analysis suggested that the replication of compositional information from one generation to the next would be too inaccurate for natural selection to work.

None of these objections, though, is about whether early life needed a bubble. Even Szostak, who favors an RNA-based explanation for the origin of life, agrees on the point that the lipid-first camp cares about most — that compartments are necessary to keep related molecules together. Without them, he said, “Darwinian evolution could not operate.”
