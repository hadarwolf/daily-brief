After three years and more than 20 rejections, I asked myself a difficult question: should I give up? The evidence seemed clear. American universities did not want me as a philosophy graduate student. The world was telling me that an academic life was nothing more than a teenage dream. My lifelong goal of becoming a professional philosopher appeared to founder on the shoals of academia. It was time to decide whether it made sense to continue pursuing what the world told me was an unachievable project. But how does one decide when it’s time to give up?

In theory, there is a tidy formula to know when to abandon a goal: when the expected utility of quitting becomes greater than the expected utility of pursuing it. But, of course, the clean theoretical answer dissolves in the strange complexities of life. When we face the question of giving up in reality, expected utility is really just an arbitrary personal estimation. The moment for giving up is messy, determined more by one’s idiosyncratic criteria and personality than anything that could reasonably be considered objective. This opens the door to further questions: what if the next try is the successful one? Am I letting all of my past efforts go to waste? What kind of person am I if I give up?

It is especially difficult to abandon goals when we are surrounded by stories of victorious grit. Popular culture adores portrayals of unlikely success, of those who experience setbacks, keep trying, refuse to surrender, and then, despite the overwhelming odds, win. ‘Losers’ and ‘quitters’ are insults in a society that prizes those who never give up. But the value of persistence not only permeates popular culture. It has also prompted moral philosophers to stress the virtues of grit.

Philosophers have recently been animated by the challenge of grasping the nature and rationality of persistence. One important thinker about persistence was the American philosopher Robert Nozick, who held that honouring our past efforts builds a commitment that helps us overcome the temptation to quit and may push us to achieve long-term goals. In a similar line of thought, Jennifer Morton and Sarah Paul invite readers to always start long-term projects with the strong belief that they will succeed despite evidence to the contrary, which in favourable circumstances can overcome deadening inertia. Others, like Elizabeth Jackson, underline hope as fundamental for rationalising the pursuit of long-term goals despite evidence of failure and temptations to give up. Philosophers thus contribute to a culture of perseverance.

Despite its virtues, an important problem of a culture of perseverance is that it hides exactly what it denies: the positive value of giving up. Grit is all well and good, but in real life, abandoning goals is crucial to discovering who we are. This is what I call the exploratory value of giving up: quitting may reveal skills, limitations and passions that could not have been known prior to failure.

To grasp the exploratory value of giving up, we must understand the process of choosing goals. Rational choice theory distinguishes between means and goals. Goals fall beyond the purview of rationality; they are chosen according to our preferences. Once chosen, goals provide the fixed point from which the means can be rationally chosen. But mere pursuit of preference is not the only way to choose goals. They can also be selected according to the means that are available through a process of exploration and improvisation. Saras Sarasvathy, a theorist of entrepreneurship, explains these two ways of choosing goals with a mundane illustration: deciding what to have for dinner. We can choose a dish from a cookbook, buy the ingredients, follow the recipe, and voilà! This is normally how we think about pursuing goals: we choose one, develop the necessary skills, and strive to achieve it. The goal determines the means. In a culture of perseverance, any goal we choose is supposed to be achievable – and, if we fail, it is because we did not try hard enough.

But there is another way to choose what to have for dinner. You can first look at the ingredients you have in the cupboard and improvise. This is a different selection process. The ingredients determine the dish. Sarasvathy holds that the second strategy is the way entrepreneurs build successful businesses: they try and fail until they figure out what they do well, and only then do they persevere. They give up on bad business ideas before choosing the right one. This framework allows us to grasp the exploratory value of giving up: we discover ‘the ingredients in our cupboard’ through our failures. Giving up is often a step toward self-knowledge.

Consider the choice of long-term goals. These goals are like unknown ingredients in the cupboard: we need to try them first to see what can be useful. What are the chances that we get the right combination of ingredients on the first try? Likewise, what are the chances that the first goal teenagers choose will fulfil them as adults? The earliest life goals one adopts are often chosen from a place of ignorance and childish illusion. But if we acknowledge that those goals are temporary and are attentive to the reasons for our failures, we are likely to gain knowledge about ourselves.

Initial goals are what the philosopher David Schmidtz, in his article ‘Choosing Ends’ (1994), calls maieutic ends, those ‘end[s] achieved through a process of coming to have other ends’. Maieutic ends are provisional and hence can orient the search for other goals. We might realise that we were not talented enough to become musicians, that we sabotage romantic relationships that feel too close to be safe, or that we do not want to spend years writing a dissertation, and so on. Being attentive to failure is being alert to one’s skills, talents and drives. The search is not easy, and it might need to restart many times throughout a lifetime, not always because the lessons are not learned, but because we change with the circumstances.

Other times what is learned from failure is not something about ourselves, but about the context of our life. An ambitious young woman from rural West Virginia realises that she lacks the means to go to college; an Indigenous writer from Chiapas who works in his mother tongue accepts that his texts will not be read by many; the chair of the literature department of a small university comes to terms with the fact that the administration is gradually eliminating the humanities. Failure helps us learn that our conditions are not right for the goal we are pursuing. Yet reality is not so malleable. When our surroundings are working against us, the right strategy is not always to change them. Sometimes it is better to change ourselves. That wisdom can be found in René Descartes’s words: ‘my … maxim was to endeavour always to conquer myself rather than fortune, and change my desires rather than the order of the world.’ Maybe that ambitious young woman from rural West Virginia must find ways to succeed that do not involve college; perhaps the writer from Chiapas needs to learn to write some texts in English; and the literature professor may need to find a way to make a living outside academia.

Consider again the original question. How do we know when one should give up? In theory, the answer is straightforward; in reality, asking for the optimal point for abandoning goals seems to be the wrong question. The right one is different: would I know myself better if I gave up? This is what ultimately helped me decide to quit trying to become an academic philosopher. I did not renew my adjunct contract at the Universidad Iberoamericana in Mexico City, and took over the old office job of a good friend. It wasn’t much, but it was a place to start reimagining my life. Then, after a week of taking the new job, I couldn’t tolerate how unmotivating the office job was. It was worse than the sting of rejections. So I confirmed that what moved me was doing philosophy. What I really had to give up on was the office job – and what I had to persist with was the PhD applications. For two more years I applied. The first year I was rejected. Then, one day, I was accepted at the University of Arizona. The risk paid off. But it might not have done. Giving up on philosophy might have led me to a better place. I doubt it, not because I persevered, but because, for one week, I did not. That is how I learned what moved me.

Friedrich Nietzsche’s aphorism ‘What does not kill me makes me stronger’ has become a cliché at the core of a culture of perseverance. But it implies an often overlooked truth: what does not make us stronger may also teach us who we are.
