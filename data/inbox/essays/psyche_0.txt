It was one of those suspended moments – I sat still, my thumb scrolling across the screen. The strange thing is this: you know no one is online, no new messages, no new stories. And, still, you scroll, in hopes that something – anything – might confirm the world you left behind hasn’t simply vanished.

Everything on my feed felt irrelevant – until, suddenly, it wasn’t.

I came across a cooking video my mother had liked before we lost the connection. Seeing her name beneath the post struck me, as if someone had brought news. My eyes filled. I read the recipe for pasta with white sauce in the caption. She must have saved it for one of those evenings when she didn’t know what to cook. I took a screenshot, as if I might need to return to it – something I didn’t want to lose: yadgari, a keepsake, a trace of her touch pressed into a screen.

After 10 years of living in India, my connection to home in Iran had settled into a rhythm. Each morning, I checked our family groups on the messaging app Telegram to see what had unfolded while I slept. During the day, a message from my mother, a brief pause in the flow of things, felt like a small gift.

At night, I scrolled through Persian Instagram – jokes, cooking videos, fragments of humour that make sense only in our language. I would laugh until I cried, then fall asleep with a quiet reassurance: somewhere, my world was still alive.

We had an almost-fixed time for a video call, the holy hour of 6 pm. My parents would already be there, waiting. Behind them, the red carpet framed on the wall; in front of them, a bowl of seasonal fruits. Sometimes my mother would say: ‘Add your sister.’

We spoke every day. Nothing new – just exchanging words, routines and meaning.

These calls – the pictures, voices, messages – were part of life, like tea

My father had established this unwritten rule: we had to talk every day, even if only for two minutes. He described it as a kind of dependency, a small but necessary interruption to the weight of his routine. Our conversations moved easily between memories, what we made of them, and laughter. Always laughter.

My mother’s connection to me was different. Our conversations were built from the small details of her day: what she cooked, what she cleaned, the news of a cousin or a neighbour. In return, I gave her the only thing I could: an audience. I listened to the rhythm of her life and offered my voice back to her: Khaste nabashi. ‘May you never be tired.’ In those moments, her daily work wasn’t just chores; it was a story we shared.

These calls – the pictures, voices, messages – were part of life, like tea, like the fading light of evening. We watched each other’s days unfold.

Then, almost within a few minutes, the flow stopped.

It was 28 February, a Saturday morning, project day at my child’s school in Bengaluru. I was holding my phone, recording my son’s presentation – already imagining sending it to my parents. Although I tried to stay present, my attention kept drifting back to the screen. For weeks, I had been checking updates and watching tensions rise in the Persian Gulf.

Then, on our family Telegram group, a message stopped me: Bacheha, jang shoro shod! ‘Guys, war has begun!’

At first, it felt unreal, like the many rumours that circulate in tense times. I checked state news channels. This time, it was confirmed. War had begun. Tehran was attacked.

I stepped out of the hall into the yard, searching for a corner of quiet. My eyes were already burning. I returned to the family group chat immediately. A shared awareness had taken over: we could lose connection at any moment. It felt as if we had been given a brief, fragile window for final words, and we all knew it.

Replies came in snatches. Then, one by one, they disappeared

I didn’t know what to say. I used the moment as if it were the last chance to speak: Kheili dosetoon daram. ‘Please take care, I love you all.’

Replies came in snatches: ‘Don’t worry about us. At least you are far from this.’ Repeated, reassuring.

A few messages noted others going offline. Then those voices vanished too. The group thinned in real time, presence giving way to absence, names turning into data.

My last message remained unseen: Kasi hast? ‘Anyone there?’

Around me, the school day continued. Parents talked, children laughed, people moved in and out of the hall. Inside that ordinary noise, the world I knew simply vanished. With my country under attack and the screens gone dark, I felt suddenly homeless. I stood there, backless and boneless. The very thing holding me upright had been pulled out from under me.

I remained on this side of the silence. At first, it felt temporary. I kept scrolling, refreshing the same apps again and again, as if my persistence could bring something back. Telegram, Instagram, news pages – nothing changed. I began searching for voices abroad instead. I joined new Telegram channels and followed unknown people on Instagram – anything to know what was happening back home.

The rhythm had changed. Mornings began with news channels: what had been attacked, which part of my city was affected, whether it was near my neighbourhood. All day, I stayed alert – phone in hand, refreshing the same pages, switching between Persian and English sources, comparing headlines, trying to understand what was real and what was not.

Days passed. News moved on to other topics, and this silence – this disconnection – seemed normal and eternal.

I stopped writing: ‘If you connect, let me know you’re OK.’

Instead, I returned to old, archived chats with my mom. The last minutes before the shutdown – I had told her not to send dad out for unnecessary shopping. She had told me: ‘If the internet goes, don’t worry about us.’

I read every comment, slowly. I cried with them without them knowing

I scrolled back. Photos of foods, new clothes, videos of my son singing in broken Persian, emojis, laughter. Baba’s voice – always joking.

I began to avoid their photos. I would open the chat, then close it before my parents’ images loaded. I didn’t know if they were still there. I couldn’t bear to see and couldn’t stay away.

I read posts by Iranians in the diaspora. I read every comment, slowly. I cried with them without them knowing.

I tried calling landlines, different operators. Nothing worked.

Until, one day, an unknown number called.

‘I didn’t believe I could reach you. We are all alive,’ she said. Her voice was thin, crackling through the distance, but it was hers.

That call felt like a gift. From then on, I answered every unknown number.

Meanwhile, nothing around me had changed. The same streets, the same routines, the same conversations, but I moved through them differently.

I found myself refreshing my phone in the middle of conversations, between sentences, even while someone was speaking. After school, I stood in the corner of the playground, phone in hand. It became a habit. Other parents learned not to approach me.

Things I had once tried to avoid now felt impossible not to do.

People asked about my family. They listened, then returned to their own concerns – not out of indifference, but because life required it. For them, it was news. For me, it was always there.

Her name confirms, for a moment, that she is there – reachable, but not really within reach

One evening, at a friend’s house, I sat playing cards and my mind drifted back to my father and uncles – their Fridays, laughter, trays of tea cups, cheating, arguments, and smoke hanging in the air.

What are they doing now? Will they ever sit together like that again?

I didn’t just miss them. I missed the world where those moments were still possible.

The shadow of war still lingers. As I write this, the silence remains.

I am no longer afraid to see my parents’ old photos. Their brief, on-and-off calls warm me.

I imagine them in the same room – the chairs, the table, the background carpet.

I go back to that screenshot. Her name beneath it confirms, for a moment, that she is there – reachable, but not really within reach.

And I keep scrolling, waiting for a grainy photo of a family dinner, a new ‘like’, or a blue checkmark – any small signal that connection is no longer fragile and the internet has returned.

I was a child when Newfoundland’s fishing collapsed. Will I see the same happen to human connection?

I lost my best friend just as motherhood began shaking the very roots of my being

With my daughters’ education cancelled, I thought the regime had done its worst. Then a new message came from my office

I knew I was committing a small social crime. But we hadn’t truly been friends for a long time

I’d reported on earthquakes, murders and suicide attacks. I was still unprepared to watch my dad die

The end of a friendship cracked me apart, triggering hidden memories – and helping me heal old wounds

My parents were gone, yet I held on to their apartment. Could I honour the past and learn to let go?

We fled war-torn Bosnia when I was three. Twenty-four years on, could I make my home there again?
