I slip my feet into my sneakers, the laces pre-tied. My coat lies in the crook of my elbow. I slide my forearm through my purse strap and lift my keys off the entryway hook, the blades clinking ever so slightly. I wince and hold my breath, praying he didn’t hear and won’t rush from the living room barking his orders, demanding I stay.

I’ve performed this routine hundreds of times, and yet adrenaline runs through my fingers like a current of electricity, paradoxically paralysing and mobilising me, as my knuckles bend around the door handle.

I tell myself go, though I’m afraid of the consequences.

You might be thinking that I live with an abusive spouse, but I don’t. I live with a dog who has separation anxiety. Some days I felt like his prisoner.

At 52, I haven’t lived a traditional woman’s life: I’ve never been married or close to engaged. I’ve never had kids, never raised a family. I’ve never allowed myself to fall in love, though not for lack of wanting.

When I was a teenager and my parents’ marital problems grew vocal and visible, relatives and friends stopped visiting. Isolation and silence overtook our home. I felt as if I’d been shipwrecked with my mother and father on a toxic abandoned island.

My mother told me she had nightmares about the house I’d grown up in

Behind closed doors, my father was abusive. Living in our house didn’t feel safe, but neither did leaving. I wanted to meet my peers for ice cream at Friendly’s, but the idea of going out gave me a stomach ache.

‘Go,’ my mother said, her glassy blue eyes dodging my gaze. ‘I’ll be fine.’

Decades later, long divorced and before her death, my mother told me she had nightmares about the house I’d grown up in: ‘He’s always there,’ she said, though my father had left and my mother had sold the house and moved. ‘I can never leave.’

Sometimes I’ll have this nightmare: I’m running from my childhood bedroom, down the stairs of our split-level suburban ranch, to the bolted front door, my heart pounding as my hand grasps, then turns, the doorknob. I pray I can escape.

When I was a girl, I begged my parents for a puppy, a companion. What I was really looking for was love. The answer was no, but my father said he could be my pretend dog. He got down on all fours and panted and pawed my body. He pushed me over and got on top of me. He assaulted me. My childhood dream of love, and family, died hard.

As an adult, I thought I could right my wrong.

Unpartnered and almost 47, during the acute loneliness of the COVID-19 pandemic I adopted a puppy: Beau was a sweet, smart, playful yellow Lab mix found under a drug house in Mississippi, though when we met I didn’t know his history. He was transported to New England, and came to live with me in my small city condo, just outside of Boston. I imagined he and I would do everything together: he’d be my jogging buddy, my backseat driver, my soul mate.

Going out on a date, or just to check the mail, was out of the question

But Beau barked and lunged at people on rollerblades and skateboards, so jogging together wasn’t feasible. In the car, when he saw scooter riders on our congested streets, he punctured the side door’s interior with his claws.

While I was at the supermarket, he self-destructed – barking, hyperventilating, and soiling himself. Going out on a date, or just to check the mail, was out of the question. My friends told me not to worry, just let him cry it out, like a baby. But Beau didn’t have run-of-the-mill separation anxiety. His, as I came to learn, was a severe clinical case requiring medication and intense training, which I diligently, doggedly pursued. I didn’t fathom that when everyone I knew had long left their pandemic quarantine, I’d still be living in relative isolation. My friends began to suggest I re-home Beau.

But I loved him. I couldn’t bear to give him up, or give up period.

Vowing to find a solution to what seemed an impossible problem, I hired a behaviourist trainer who specialised in separation anxiety. We spent 10 months on pre-departure cue desensitisation – approach the door; put on your coat; put on your shoes, pick up your keys – and 18 months on leaving the premises with Beau home by himself for a few seconds, working our way to 20 minutes.

The first time I left my building without Beau, the trainer watched the pet camera video feed for me from her home, so that I could take a walk without constant monitoring.

‘I’m at the bakery,’ I texted the trainer as the smell of coffee, chocolate croissants, pecan muffins and cheesecake brownies awakened my desire to be out in the world, to live. I felt anxious and eyed the clock. I might not have time to wait my turn. ‘There’s a line. Should I stay or go home?’

‘Stay,’ the trainer replied. ‘Beau’s calm. He’s lying on the floor with his eyes closed.’

For an entire year his anxiety regressed to the point I couldn’t leave him alone

When I reached the front of the line, I ordered not one but a handful of baked goods, thinking I’d better stock up since I wasn’t sure when or if I’d be able to come again. As the cashier handed me my bag-to-go, I felt as if I was both receiving an Olympic medal and robbing a bank.

A simple outing felt both extraordinary and forbidden.

Twenty minutes solo eventually led to 45.

Just when I thought we were almost home free, the unthinkable happened: while I was at work, Beau was attacked at doggie daycare. He needed emergency surgery to repair the physical damage. His emotional wounds were more complicated to heal, and for an entire year his anxiety regressed to the point I couldn’t leave him alone. He panicked with sitters, even people he knew well. Daycare was no longer an option. What was I going to do?

Beau’s assault and the aftermath called up my past, the event I thought I’d put behind me: the violation, the betrayal, the fear that I might never recover, that all hope, that love, was lost.

I didn’t know if I was in denial or just being persistent, but I didn’t quit. Love fuelled me.

Slowly, we climbed our way back to 20-minute absences, then one hour, then two hours. Through it all, I kept waiting for everything to fall apart again. I kept looking for the sign that said I didn’t deserve my dog or my freedom, or anyone’s love.

It’s March 2025, and I turn on the television and also a calming instrumental music station, having discovered that not one but two layers of sound help block out the city and building noises, and I leave the condo, locking the door behind me, walking down the hallway, toward the building stairwell, watching Beau via the pet camera video feed on my phone.

I pray Beau will self-soothe but as I get into my car, his barking escalates

Hypervigilance is a symptom of my PTSD, and Beau’s. I watch as he trots his 80-pound body to the entryway, which he usually does after I exit and before he returns to the couch, his ‘go-to’ spot, which he selected for himself over our almost four years of separation-anxiety training.

But today he doesn’t go to his spot. Something triggers him – a noise, a sense, something. He fixes his eyes on the door. His ears bend forward. He stands erect, staring in the direction of where I’ve gone. His jaw tightens.

I start sweating. I can’t return – I’m scheduled to teach a Zumba class at a local gym, my hard-won part-time gig that I did for fun and some extra money, a job I almost lost when I was housebound after we lost daycare. I pray Beau will self-soothe but as I get into my car, his barking escalates. I feel powerless to the destruction of what I’ve built, a house of cards caving in. I drive to work, my palms clammy. I’m afraid of an irreversible regression. I’m afraid of getting fined for a neighbour’s complaint. I’m afraid of seeing the truth: that I’m still caught in the grip of my past, and I don’t know the way out.

For years, I hid my body underneath layers of baggy clothes. When I was 40, I watched a Zumba class from a treadmill outside the dance studio, telling myself I could never do those moves, could never let go of my fear of being in the world – of being seen.

Now, 11 years later, I’m standing on the studio stage, wearing neon spandex pants and a lacy sports bra, shaking my booty, leading a Latin-inspired dance fitness class while my phone blinks with barking notifications. I don’t stop dancing, even as anxiety chews my gut, spitting out the question: how will I bear this disaster?

‘Disaster?’ my therapist will say at my next session. I know it’s an extreme word to characterise a dog anxiously barking. Did Beau harm himself or my home in my absence? No. Did anyone get hurt or die? No.

It will be the worst day for separation anxiety in two years, but it won’t be disastrous. By the time my class ends, the dog walker will have picked up Beau, texting ‘he’s fine’.

Later, I’ll review the footage: 21 barking incidents, one almost a minute long, the others 15-30 seconds. I won’t discount the three 7-10 minute intervals when Beau napped on the couch. I’ll remind myself that a really bad day doesn’t mean everything is lost.

After 1,000 separation anxiety sessions, I stopped training Beau and started living.
