The day we left Brooklyn felt like the great escape. My family had lived there since my great-grandparents fled the pogroms of eastern Europe. By the time we pulled up stakes, we hardly had a choice.

At first, our cluster of 19 buildings in an East New York housing project had felt like a little town; we walked safely everywhere, just us kids. But by 1970 the streets were dangerous. A girl was raped on our roof. My best friend’s father was stabbed. Most of our neighbours had left, and we were going, too. Just like that, I lost friends I’d had since toddlerhood and the place where I’d formed my dreams.

In our new ‘luxe digs’ in Co-op City in the Bronx, I carried the world that made me: the asphalt park called the Circle, where I raced on roller skates tightened by key; nights on concrete barrels, transistor radios straining with Stevie Wonder and the early Stones. Through later moves from Boston to LA, I kept an eye on my old projects, a dream in sepia and an omen that life could upend.

I watched fake-posh Brooklyn rise from the mist. Its newbies botched the egg cream, our fountain drink, but the cafés where I met writers had charm. Now it’s time to move and I’m heading back to Brooklyn. We’ll live across from the Central Library and Prospect Park. This past Halloween, visiting our soon-to-be neighbourhood, I found streets closed to traffic, families in costume painting pumpkins and collecting treats – like scenes from when I was young. A lost home lives on in spikes of joy and longing; it shifts in memory as our lives change. You can try to go home again, but the home you return to is never the place in your mind.

There’s a joke about ageing: one day, you no longer fall over, you ‘have a fall’. I’m not there yet, but when I tripped on a step recently, it wasn’t elegant. My ankle made an audible pop, and I hit the ground.

I’ve broken bones before, but this time the hardest part of recovery has been how it’s affected my sense of self. In recent years, I’ve built part of my identity around running, so being told to stop – for months – was a blow. Now when people ask if I’m a runner, I’m not sure what to say.

I’m just an amateur, but it turns out that injured athletes at every level go through something similar. Unable to compete, they often lose the personal and social structures their identity is built upon, leaving them particularly vulnerable to mental health struggles.

So how to respond? A clue comes from a study of retiring athletes. Those whose identity and social relationships rested solely on their sport struggled most. ‘Retirement entailed not just a loss of a sense of “me”,’ the researchers write, ‘but also a loss of a sense of “we” and “us”.’

Those with social ties and activities beyond their sport coped better. They managed to ‘remoor’ their identity, anchoring it somewhere new and meaningful, like study or other communities, while also keeping a thread to the old – by coaching, say, or staying close to teammates. As one put it: ‘I’m more than my sport.’

Unlike the people I’ve been reading about, I will return to running, but there will be a day when I won’t. So this pause has made me reflect on how much of my identity rests on one activity. Next time I fall – or have a fall – I want more than one thing to catch me.

For advice on training, wellbeing and more, I highly recommend the Running Channel podcast. It’s welcoming and accessible for runners of all abilities.

And if you are doubtful of running’s benefits, try the Psyche Guide ‘How to Enjoy Running’ (2022) by Christian Jarrett.

Like many people nowadays, I grew up learning to see the sun as a threat. Exposure to its rays meant consequences like premature wrinkles, sunspots and, of course, skin cancer. The Korean aunties I knew heeded this dogma with sunglasses, flared visor hats, parasols and sun sleeves. While I’ve never gone to such lengths, I internalised the message.

But in his latest book In Defense of Sunlight (2026), the journalist Rowan Jacobsen argues that the common aversion to the sun has gone too far. He lays out decades of research linking moderate amounts of outdoor sunlight exposure with various positive health outcomes. One especially surprising study – which followed almost 30,000 Swedish women over 20 years – suggested that avoiding sunlight was about as big a risk factor for death as smoking. Sunlight is thought to aid physical health by, among other things, stimulating the production of vitamin D as well as the release of compounds such as nitric oxide (which can lower blood pressure).

Then there are the mental health benefits. Sun on the skin helps the body release endorphins, its natural opioids, and might help protect against depression. And proper exposure to sunlight and darkness massages the circadian rhythm into regularity, improving sleep quality, which, in turn, benefits your wellbeing.

Of course, everything in moderation: we still need to protect ourselves from excessive sun and UV exposure (so don’t throw away your sunscreen). But Jacobsen’s writing has helped me see how too much fear of the sun might be harming rather than protecting my health.

Now, the trick is to strike a balance, which I’ve sought by listening to my body. This past summer I tried to embrace the sun, retreating to the shade when I felt overheated or overstimulated. Maintaining moderation takes some push and pull, but I try to follow Jacobsen’s bottom-line message: ‘Get sun. Not too much. Go outside.’

For more on how the sun looms large in our minds, you can read Karl Ove Knausgaard’s ‘An Ode to the Sun’ (2017) on Literary Hub. This short piece meditates on the sun’s godlike power, its indifference to life and its inevitability.

While I’ve come across various literary and artistic works praising the art of looking, what truly led me down this path was Nirmal Verma’s Ek Chithda Sukh (1979), translated as A Rag Called Happiness (1993). In this Hindi novel, the 14-year-old narrator Munnu writes moving descriptions of everyday life in his notebook: ‘This I’ll remember, the boy told himself; I’ll put it down in my diary. Bitty was hanging clothes out to dry. A pale light filled the room. Darry sat back against the wall, his eyes on Bitty …’

Under its influence, I began to narrativise the world around me, so that my journal entries became ready reckoners for the future me: I’d simply page through for what was happening in my life outside the usual things we remember – family, friends, work, city living.

I catalogued the ephemera that tend to slip between the days and the headlines. The dreams I had and probably hated; my thoughts about a bitter cold in the middle of summer; my anxiety before a job interview; the excitement of watching a movie that overwhelmed me.

These are details we tend to gloss over in a 24-hour cycle, but in aggregate they accrue meaning – they slow me down, make me more observant. They denote a sense of time and the self, coming together in a way their digital counterparts don’t. It helps that I journal with pen or pencil, not phone or app, the better to etch the small things in my mind.

At the end of A Rag, all Munnu has are these recollections of times spent with Bitty and her gang of theatre friends over the summer. The break, the year, all these people, pass him by so simply, but the filled-up pages of his notebook, all the observations he has amassed, will stay forever as markings of a time lived – well or not, only time can tell.

To read more about Verma and his works, read the essay ‘A Patch of Sunlight: Remembering Nirmal Verma’ (2017) by Priyanka Dubey in The Caravan magazine.

I will confess that, when it comes to sports, I am a fair-weather fan. I like basketball but had little interest in the New York Knicks until they made it to the NBA finals in June. I’m barely interested in soccer unless the Canadian team or the Korean team make it at least to the World Cup round of 32. I root for teams when there’s cause to hope and, when they do advance, I’m as joyful and riotous as any fan.

My intermittent fandom used to be a source of sheepishness. It’s easy to feel, or be made to feel, like a fake fan or a poseur. Especially if you’re among ‘real’, lifelong fans. But this year, I’ve learned to embrace fair-weather fandom as a legitimate way to join in with a collective experience. While there will always be someone out there who might grumble about my fan status or try to flex their superior knowledge of player history, I’ve found that most people are just happy you’re celebrating with them – that you’ve come to recognise a team’s greatness.

Jumping on bandwagons is a normal and valuable part of life, really. We all use cues from the people around us to decide when and where to shift our attention. And if, for a brief window of time, we turn that gaze to a sports team we wouldn’t ordinarily focus on, that’s our prerogative. After all, watching sports can be really good for our psychological wellbeing – in part, research suggests, because of the social connection it promotes as we watch along with other fans. I particularly love those moments when a crowd of like-minded fans grows tense, hearts all racing together, until our team pulls through and we erupt into roars. When there’s an opportunity to participate in that shared drama and excitement, we should seize it.

Rooting for a sports team with others is also one of the activities that can bring more awe into your life, per the Psyche Guide ‘How to Experience More Wow’ (2021) by Summer Allen.
