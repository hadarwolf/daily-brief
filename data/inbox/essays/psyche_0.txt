In 2008, Bob Nygaard sat on a stool at the Neighborhood Sports Bar and Grill in Boca Raton, Florida, just before 5 pm. It was Wednesday, a night with generous happy-hour deals and the possibility of taking someone home by last call. Nygaard, a tall and brawny man with a disarmingly goofy smile, had the strongest New York accent for miles. He had recently retired from a 21-year-long career as an NYPD officer. At 46, he got out when his pension kicked in, and moved down to Florida to relax.

Nygaard had been a high-energy cop who didn’t get along with fellow officers because of how doggedly he pursued arrests. He probably should have known that he wouldn’t adjust to sipping Mai Tais and sitting on the beach. Shortly after arriving in Florida, he got a private investigator’s licence, using it to track down missing money or catch cheating husbands. Neither was satisfying.

He ordered a rum and Coke. He had become friendly with the bartender, who introduced him to two women sitting at the end of the bar, a nurse and a doctor. They had dinner together, swapping stories about working in medicine and the police force. Nygaard gave both women his card. Ten minutes later, his phone rang. It was the doctor. Could they meet up nearby, on a street corner outside a Chevron station? Nygaard thought she was hoping to hook up.

When she pulled up and rolled down the window, her face was half-covered in shadows. ‘I’ve never been able to tell anyone this before,’ she said from the driver’s seat. ‘I’m a medical doctor and I fell for a psychic.’ She had given more than $12,000 to a woman she met through her hairdresser. The psychic said someone had put a curse on the doctor: it explained why she was having issues with her immigration papers, and why her love life was ruined. For enough money, the curse could be lifted. Now, the doctor realised she’d been had. Nygaard had arrested conmen before. Could he help her?

From that moment on, Nygaard was never bored again. He became a PI who specialises in helping victims of psychics. And he didn’t settle cases out of court – he went after arrests to get as much money back for his clients as possible. Through word of mouth, psychic victims all over the country have sought him out. Since his first case, Nygaard has helped recover more than $5 million.

I first heard about Nygaard around a year ago, through a news article about a psychic’s arrest he had orchestrated in Philadelphia. I thought of him as part fictional detective Sherlock Holmes, part real-life Harry Houdini, who spent his later years exposing mediums. When I initially reached out over email, Nygaard didn’t respond. But after I sent a follow-up, I got a call from an unknown number a few days later. It was Nygaard: ‘I like when people don’t give up,’ he told me. He started talking fast, recounting his recent cases. What drives a retired cop, someone who busted drug dealers and burglars, to focus only on this fringe sect of society – psychics? And why was he so good at it? We made plans to meet up in the city.

On a below-freezing day in early February, I turned a narrow corner in lower Manhattan and saw a figure wearing a beige trench coat and fedora standing under the granite arch of the historic Life and Trust Bank. In my memory, steam started to rise up from the sidewalk grates, and the deserted street became the spot for a clandestine midnight meet-up. In truth, it was a clear sunny morning and Nygaard was politely waiting for me outside, but every minute spent with him can make you feel like you’re acting out a scene from a movie set in old New York. The former bank now houses a coffee shop. We walked into the cavernous lobby and ordered drinks where the bank tellers once sat.

I expected to meet a hardened law-enforcement officer with a dark past. Maybe he, or someone he loved, had been wronged by a psychic, and he had a score to settle. Or maybe he was a rationalist – someone so dedicated to an evidence-based view of the world that he wouldn’t stand for anyone proposing they could break the laws of nature.

But as we settled down with our hot drinks, I learned that Nygaard hadn’t thought about psychics at all until later in life. He had a happy, grounded childhood, he told me. He was born in Queens in 1961, and moved to New Hyde Park, Long Island, when he was four. His mother was 20 when she had him, his father only a few years older. The family, including his younger brother, ate dinner together every evening.

‘Knowing how to deal with the raccoon also helps you deal with the person’

They were a hard-working and proud unit. The family would put on their best clothes to go to the Howard Johnson’s on Jericho Turnpike for fish and chips. Nygaard was taught to always be ‘squared away’. He made his bed in the morning. His hair was always combed. ‘You pay attention to the details, to the little things,’ he said. ‘You’re clean, well dressed, polite, you’re on time.’ Even though his apartment was just around the corner from the café where we sat, he had arrived nearly 20 minutes early to make sure he wasn’t late. (Nygaard now splits his time between Florida and New York, based largely on when the New York Rangers are playing; he has season tickets.)

His sense of justice also seems to have started early. If Nygaard came home and said that all the kids were picking on someone in the schoolyard – ‘knocking them on the ground, calling him four-eyes’ – Nygaard’s father would counter: ‘You didn’t sit there and watch, did you?’

Nygaard spent summers in northeast Pennsylvania, where his grandfather had bought an old farmhouse with no running water or electricity; it relied on kerosene lamps and a well. The property reminded his grandfather of Norway. When Nygaard brought out his friends, brothers called Tommy and Richy Gambino, they had never seen a cow before. Once, they tricked another visiting city kid by telling him that chocolate milk came out of the brown cows’ udders.

In the countryside, Nygaard fostered compassion for other living things. When he was around six, he had a BB gun to shoot at cans. One day, he saw a bird, aimed his gun at it, and shot it out of a tree. Nygaard looked at it up close, examining the pellet in its belly. It wasn’t dead, but it was suffering. He killed it, to put it out of its misery, and then felt sick. ‘I never killed again,’ he told me. Yet, he also gained insights into the animalistic side of humans. When he saw a raccoon trapped inside the barn, he later recognised similar responses in perps with a warrant on them, cornered on a subway platform. ‘Knowing how to deal with the raccoon also helps you deal with the person,’ he said.

It was Nygaard’s father who wanted him to be a police officer. When Nygaard was 17, he took the police test. The job came with a pension and healthcare, Nygaard’s father would say. Plus, he could help people. But Nygaard went to college in Texas, at Southern Methodist University, majoring in business – thinking he would join the family flooring company. Then he got called to the police academy at 23.

Police work matched his philosophy of justice: when someone broke the law, there were consequences, and it was his job to deliver them. He became obsessive about making arrests, so much so that he chafed against the other cops. When he was an officer on Long Island, there were unspoken rules about how proactive or ambitious to be, he said. Others would accuse him of creating too much paperwork. Supervisors punished him by putting him on desk duty. Yet he still managed to make arrests without leaving the station, remembering passersby with outstanding warrants, and once discovering drugs in someone’s purse. He memorised the penal code, so that he could always justify what he was doing, including a New York State law that fortune-telling was a crime.

One of Nygaard’s few police mentors was the late Jon Grow, a detective at the Baltimore Police Department. Grow co-founded the National Association of Bunco Investigators, a non-profit that helped law-enforcement officers share information about fraudsters and scams around the country. Grow passed Nygaard names and photos of scammers who had hit nearby cities. But it ‘wasn’t until that woman in that bar [in Florida] … that all of that knowledge from those years came into play,’ he said.

When the doctor asked Nygaard to help her, he asked if the psychic’s last name was Marks. Stunned, the doctor asked how he had known. ‘I’m psychic,’ he joked. Actually, the psychic in question was Gina Marie Marks, a serial offender whose last name Nygaard remembered from his conversations with Grow.

Nygaard had one stipulation for taking the doctor’s case: she would pursue an arrest no matter how much money the psychic offered her to settle. Then he found four more women who were Marks’s victims, and gathered evidence from all of them. Eventually, Marks was arrested, and the doctor got her money back.

Marks was his first psychic arrest, and that was the moment the disparate pieces of his life clicked into place. ‘All of those years, all that aggravation … fighting with the bosses … having to know the law – I never understood what it was forging me for,’ he said. ‘It was forging me for this fight.’

In early March, Nygaard and I ate dinner at a corner table at P J Clarke’s in midtown Manhattan. He was telling me about another case: in June 2010, a young woman – let’s use the name Vivian – was walking in New York City when she was approached by a woman who called herself Sarah. Sarah said she sensed an energy from Vivian and that something bad was happening in her life. Indeed, Vivian had recently been discharged from Army Officer Candidate School after an injury, and was going through a divorce.

Sarah and Vivian exchanged numbers, and Sarah later told her that she needed $90 for crystals to figure out where the negativity was coming from. By the end of the month, Sarah said she had figured it out: someone had placed a curse on Vivian, probably her stepmother. Vivian should take out $2,700 and put the money, along with rose petals and tree leaves, under her mattress and sleep on it.

Later, Sarah contacted Vivian to confirm it was her stepmother who had placed the curse. To lift it, she would have to sacrifice the $2,700. Vivian needed to bring the money, a jar, and a pair of white underwear to her apartment. Sarah filled the jar with water, wrapped an egg in the underwear, and rubbed it over Vivian’s body. In the dramatic finale of the ritual, the egg cracked open, and a dead baby corn snake fell out. The water had turned a deep, bloody red.

Psychic tricks haven’t changed much, Nygaard said. Needles emerge from a tomato. An egg cracks, and blood or dead animals spill out

Over the succeeding months, Sarah asked Vivian to send more money. In October 2013, Sarah said ‘the church’ needed $10,800, and Vivian went to the bank and took out a $12,000 loan. Vivian followed instructions to send Sarah a Cartier watch and Saks Fifth Avenue gift cards, though she was unsuccessful in leasing a Mercedes. By the time Vivian contacted Nygaard, she had lost more than $30,000.

Nygaard took Vivian’s case to the NYPD, where he was turned away. But Nygaard knew that Sarah had asked Vivian to send a watch to her address in Florida. If any part of a crime happens out of state, the whole crime can be prosecuted in that jurisdiction. Nygaard flew there to speak to a detective who agreed to help. His accumulation of evidence led to Sarah’s arrest, and Vivian’s money was returned.

Psychic tricks haven’t changed much, Nygaard told me. Needles emerge from a tomato. Something ominous comes out of a grapefruit. An egg cracks, and blood or dead animals spill out. ‘A lot of the newer ones, they stay away from that stuff, but when they want to really go for it, they go old school,’ Nygaard said.

As Nygaard ate his burger, I shared a case of my own: I had gone to see Sarah, who now goes by Emma, a couple weeks earlier, for a psychic reading. Nygaard perked up: ‘Oh, you did?’

I had nervously walked down 12th Street in the East Village until I arrived at a storefront with a green sign. I had wondered if Emma would try to scare me with a curse or some other tactic to get more money from me. I asked for a full-life reading for $150. I didn’t want to lie to be a better mark, so I brought up the most vulnerable topic I had on hand: whether my husband and I should try to have a baby. Emma looked carefully at me while I sat somewhat awkwardly in a large, velvet throne-like chair.

She asked some leading questions about my family (‘there is one family member you’re not as close to, there’s some pain there’) and my husband (‘you’re not spending as much time together these days’). Her first insight was universally obvious, and the second was untrue. I tried to keep the conversation going, but we naturally petered out around 25 minutes later. Emma asked if I would like to have my aura cleansed, because she saw I had two dark spots; it would cost an additional $250. I had budgeted only $300. I declined. Looking a little bored, Emma ended the session.

I found the interaction unimpressive, and, as I left, my sympathy for Nygaard’s clients wavered. The experience didn’t feel like a film noir; I wasn’t caught up in a seedy underbelly world of conmen who were experts at deception. The storefront space had been dumpy, and I was embarrassed to be there. I was even embarrassed for Emma, who was barefoot, possibly wearing a nightgown, and had put no effort into the reading. There were clearly children hiding behind a curtain, attempting to be quiet while their mom worked.

As a journalist who writes about science, I have covered my share of health scams. Throughout my career, I’ve spent hours carefully explaining in articles why certain wellness products don’t work and are a waste of money. There’s no scarcity of psychic scams either. Recent surveys suggest that four in 10 US adults believe in psychics, and as of 2022 the industry makes around $2 billion a year. After a few years of debunking, I accepted that scams were like a hydra’s heads: cut one off, and two more grew in its place.

Walking down the sidewalk, I hardened against the people who could fall for what I’d just experienced. If people could get tricked by that, what good was it to help them?

Across the street from P J Clarke’s, at the New York Public Library for the Performing Arts, I had tickets to a one-night-only performance of Houdini Speaks to the Living, a play based on letters between Harry Houdini and Sir Arthur Conan Doyle. Nygaard and I crossed the street into a packed auditorium, finding two empty seats near the back. The lights dimmed, and two actors walked on stage, one representing Doyle, and the other Houdini.

Doyle had dreamed up Sherlock Holmes, the ultimate logician who solved crimes by piecing together clues in ingenious ways. But Doyle was an advocate of Spiritualism, the belief that mediums could communicate with the dead, while Houdini (who once brought tricks based on the idea to his performance) came to believe that the profession was poison. In 1926, Houdini testified before the US Congress for a law that would criminalise fortune-telling. Elected officials didn’t feel it was necessarily their responsibility to help those who were duped. (Did I relate?) Ralph Gilbert, the representative for Kentucky, said that Houdini was ‘taking the matter entirely too seriously’.

The play dramatised Doyle’s attempts to convince Houdini that some mediums were credible. Houdini rebutted with counter-Spiritualism screeds, revealing how the mediums’ tricks work. He was harsh, and came across as a self-righteous know-it-all. Doyle and Houdini’s friendship ended, and they never spoke again.

At the end of the show, Nygaard was animated. I expected him to align with Houdini, especially after my encounter with Emma. Nygaard had seemed like an obvious reincarnation of the magician and his undertaking. Others think so too – Nygaard was recently invited to the Society of American Magicians’ Paranormal Investigations Committee, and regularly speaks at sceptic societies.

‘When the right con artist comes along and uses the right techniques on you … It can be more powerful than a gun’

But Nygaard felt conflicted. ‘My investigations are really not about supernatural ability,’ he said. He doesn’t care whether people can see the future. ‘I’ve never gone after a psychic and said … they should be arrested because they say they’re psychic,’ he said. He busts people for lying about what they say they’re doing with their clients’ money: buying cars and watches instead of casting spells.

We left the theatre and walked through a cold wind towards the 1 Train. ‘I’m going to take you out [to] the left field,’ he said. Nygaard told me he’s had experiences that have made him wonder about the supernatural. Sometimes, when driving a police car, he felt like the universe was taking the wheel. Once, while searching for a stolen briefcase, Nygaard heard a voice that told him to look up. He was passing by a deli, and stopped to check if the briefcase was on the roof; it was. In another instance, he was at the Chinese restaurant Wo Hop in Chinatown at 2 am when he saw, written on a dollar bill stuck to the wall, the nickname of a person who was connected to a crime he was investigating. ‘I have asked for help, and I’ve gotten answers, and I know those answers came from the universe,’ he said.

Nygaard had deep empathy for Doyle, who had lost a son in the war and wanted to speak to him again. Doyle’s grief fuelled his myopia when it came to a medium’s tricks, and when Houdini showed him it was all an illusion, it served as a reminder that his son would never return. For Nygaard, it was a perfect example of how ‘all of that learning, all of that knowledge, could go right out the window when the right con artist comes along and uses the right techniques on you … that’s how scary it is, how words can be so deceptive. It can be more powerful than a gun.’

I suddenly felt ashamed of how I felt when I left Emma. My attention had been misdirected, I realised. In being so judgmental about how someone could succumb to a bad trick, I had forgotten how much power the deceiver had.

The final moments of the show played recorded audio of Bess’s shaky voice. My stomach had tangled up into knots.

In May, Nygaard and I sat outside the Fanelli Cafe in SoHo, sharing a plate of fries in the afternoon sun.

I asked about his future. He works mostly by himself and has more cases than he can handle. He doesn’t think he could train someone to do what he does. Others might have the same investigative skills, but no one else would give psychic victims their all. He flies to other states to see clients or track psychics at the drop of a hat. He pressures police officers and prosecutors, and isn’t afraid to get in their face. His forcefulness has had consequences: in 2019, the National Association of Bunco Investigators rescinded his membership after his criticism of police handling of psychic fraud.

His dedication has required sacrifices. ‘I always did want a family, but I think what I do came at a cost,’ he said. When he was a cop, he was once on call with an officer who didn’t want to pursue an arrest because he would be late to his children’s dentist appointment. Nygaard responded: ‘Well, I guess your kids aren’t going to the dentist.’

‘You can’t have that attitude and have a relationship,’ he told me.

‘I was sitting all by myself in Florida contemplating on whether I have chosen the correct path in life’

Had I seen the movie Heat (1995), he asked, with Robert De Niro as a thief and Al Pacino as a cop? The two men are locked in a cat-and-mouse game. Near the end of the movie, they sit in a diner, comparing their lives.

‘So you never wanted a regular-type life?’ Pacino asks. He himself barely sees his family because he spends ‘all my time chasing guys like you around the block.’

De Niro replies: ‘A guy told me one time, don’t let yourself get attached to anything you are not willing to walk out on in 30 seconds flat if you feel the heat around a corner.’

That’s how Nygaard felt: ‘You want it, but you want the other thing more.’

Around a month later, on Father’s Day, I thought about Nygaard. I wondered if comparing his life with film characters who had simplistic moral drives was masking a deeper, more complex loss. The next day, I got a text from him. After spending Father’s Day alone, he had pondered the same: ‘I was sitting all by myself in Florida contemplating on whether I have chosen the correct path in life,’ he wrote. Was it all worth it?

Then, he received a message from a former client. ‘I don’t recall if you have children, but today I wanted to honour you all the same,’ she wrote. ‘You came into my life at such a strange time and the strength you provided, the direction, and how much you believed in me really made a difference and a massive impact on my life.’ Her text felt like a divine signal, he told me. ‘I felt like, through her, God was telling me: “Don’t doubt for a minute the path you have taken,”’ he wrote, ‘“because it’s the right one.”’

It was just like her – my bold, dead mother – to show up in my life again. Or was grief playing tricks on me?
