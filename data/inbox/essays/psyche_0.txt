In my clinical and research work, I have heard versions of the same story many times. A Black man comes into the therapy room saying he is sleeping poorly. He feels irritable. He has started withdrawing from people he cares about because he does not want to keep explaining why ordinary situations leave him so tense.

At first, it sounds a lot like everyday stress. But then he goes on to describe a supervisor who repeatedly questions his competence, coworkers who describe his tone as aggressive when he speaks directly, or a store employee who follows him aisle by aisle. He may mention being stopped by police years earlier, watching another Black man’s death replayed online, or worrying that his son will one day have to learn the same rules of self-protection.

Many clinicians might still hear general anxiety, anger, depression, guardedness or poor engagement. Those words may describe part of what is visible. But I believe that this misses the serious chronic injury underneath. What looks like emotional shutdown is often a survival strategy. What looks like anger is often the surface of grief, shame or exhaustion. And what looks like mistrust may be an accurate memory of institutions that have not been safe. In other words, the man’s feelings and behaviours (and those of many other Black men like him) are an understandable response to racial trauma.

Society has become increasingly comfortable talking about trauma. The term now appears everywhere, applied to experiences ranging from acute violence to everyday stress. This expanded attention has brought greater awareness to suffering that was once ignored, which is welcome. But I’m conscious that it has also led to concerns: if everything is trauma, does the concept lose its meaning?

I understand these reservations. Not every painful or unjust experience is traumatic. But I’m not arguing that all experiences of racism qualify as trauma. Mine is a narrower claim: that sustained exposure to racialised threat, dehumanisation and constraint across multiple domains of life can produce a form of chronic psychological injury that remains widely misrecognised, particularly in Black men.

Any discussion of racial trauma must acknowledge that Black women also experience profound and persistent racial harm. My focus on Black men is not an attempt to rank suffering. Instead, my aim is to draw attention to the specific pattern of exposure and misrecognition that affects Black men. Harmful stereotypes about Blackness are not gender-neutral. They are often organised through Black maleness, especially through associations with threat, danger, criminality and aggression. This means that Black male distress can be misread not only because it is racialised, but because it is interpreted through stereotypes that already cast Black men as dangerous or difficult to manage.

Black men are exposed to a gendered form of anti-Blackness that marks them as threatening, punishable and economically disposable

In a 2020 Gallup survey, Black men in the US were more than twice as likely as Black women to say that people acted afraid of them (27 per cent, compared with 11 per cent) and were also more likely to report being treated with less courtesy and viewed as dishonest. National mortality research estimates that Black men and boys face about a one in 1,000 lifetime risk of being killed by police, roughly 2.5 times the risk for white men and boys. Economic data show a different but related pattern: in their 2019 study of nearly the entire US population, the Harvard economist Raj Chetty and colleagues show that the Black/white income gap, after accounting for parental income, was driven by differences in wages and employment among Black men, and Black boys had lower incomes in adulthood than white boys in 99 per cent of Census tracts.

Taken together, these and other findings suggest that Black men are not only exposed to anti-Black racism in general; they are exposed to a gendered form of anti-Blackness that marks them as threatening, punishable and economically disposable. Unlike the kind of distressing and harmful events that are traditionally associated with post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), these constraints on Black men are not about any single event; they are structural and persistent. These conditions create a chronic threatening environment, where control over that threat is limited, and exposure to it is ongoing.

This constant exposure to threat produces a distinct kind of chronic psychological strain rather than a single episodic shock. Black men respond understandably with vigilance, guardedness, anger, emotional withdrawal or exhaustion.

Yet wider society mostly fails to recognise Black men’s experiences as racial trauma. And it rarely reads Black men’s behaviours as a necessary coping response.

Black men can be perceived more negatively than other race/gender groups on several stereotype dimensions. In their study of gendered racial stereotypes, the Princeton political scientists Corrine McConnaughy and Ismail White found that white respondents were especially likely to view Black men as violent and promiscuous, and least likely to view them as nurturing. These perceptions operate quickly and often outside conscious awareness, shaping attention, interpretation and response. This is not simply a matter of prejudice or dislike. It involves a culturally conditioned fear response that shapes how Black men are perceived and treated across contexts. Many people experience this fear as automatic and self-protective, which makes them less likely to question it.

This misrecognition has particular consequences. Anti-Black misandry affects how Black male emotion is interpreted across ordinary and institutional settings. A Black man’s guardedness may be read as hostility. His anger may be read as danger rather than as a response to repeated violation. His mistrust may be treated as paranoia or noncompliance rather than as a memory of institutions that have not been safe. In clinical settings, these readings can shape diagnosis and treatment. Hypervigilance, irritability, emotional shutdown or reluctance to disclose may be interpreted as aggression, oppositionality, poor engagement or pathology rather than as adaptations to chronic racial stress. The injury is compounded when trauma responses are interpreted through stereotypes that make Black men appear threatening rather than injured, difficult to manage rather than wounded.

The result is a paradox: the very conditions that produce injury also make that injury difficult for clinicians and wider society to recognise. Black men express their distress in ways that are easily misinterpreted, and therefore more likely to be managed as a problem than understood as a response to harm. The effect is cumulative. Distress is repeatedly misrecognised as threat. Coping strategies are repeatedly interpreted as pathology.

As a result, a Black man may have to make his suffering appear calm, reasonable and nonthreatening before it can be recognised as suffering at all.

Consider the man I described in my opening vignette. By the time he enters therapy, he may already have learned to monitor his voice, posture, facial expression and emotional range. At work, he may know that directness can be called aggression. In public, he may know that ordinary movement can attract suspicion. In medical or mental health settings, he may know that mistrust can be treated as pathology rather than as a response to institutions that have historically failed Black people. None of these moments alone has to explain everything. Over time, their cumulative effect can be more profound than any single incident might suggest.

Importantly, these dynamics – between the threats faced by Black men and their coping responses – are not well explained by appeals to masculinity norms. Black men themselves do not primarily describe their experiences in terms of performing masculinity. Instead, their concerns often reflect something more basic: a claim to dignity, to safety, and to full recognition as human beings. In this sense, the issue is not masculinity, but manhood in a humanistic and existential sense. It is about the conditions under which a person is allowed to exist without being treated as a threat.

Although the dynamics affecting Black men are especially pronounced in the United States, similar patterns of racialised threat perception and differential treatment can be observed in other contexts, making this both a national and a broader social phenomenon. For instance, young Black men in the UK’s capital were 17 times more likely to be stopped and searched by the police compared with the general population, according to analysis by the security and crime scholar Matt Ashby at University College London in 2022; and the UK government’s Lammy Review in 2017 found that Black men were 41 per cent more likely than white men to receive a prison sentence for comparable drug offences at Crown Court.

I hope by drawing attention to the racial trauma experienced by many Black men, wider society will begin to change its interpretation of behaviours that too often are seen as problematic rather than as an understandable response to chronic threat. My hope is for vigilance to no longer be mistaken for paranoia. For anger to no longer be read solely as aggression. And for withdrawal to no longer be treated as disengagement.

For clinicians like me, this means shifting from asking ‘What is wrong with this person?’ to asking ‘What conditions might have made their response necessary?’

For others, it requires a similar shift in perception. Recognising racial trauma in Black men does not require you to become a clinician. It requires a different kind of attention in everyday life. When a Black man appears guarded, angry, quiet, mistrustful or withdrawn, the first question should not be what is wrong with him, but what has he had to carry, anticipate or survive. The task is to resist the reflex that turns Black male pain into threat, and to make room for that pain to be seen as pain.

Until racial trauma in Black men is seen clearly, psychology and society as a whole will continue to overlook a form of injury that is both pervasive and deeply consequential.
