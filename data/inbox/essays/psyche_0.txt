Not long ago, a group of East African writers gathered at my house in Nairobi for a writing weekend. We were all working on manuscripts – novels set in Kenya and in Kazakhstan, histories of people in Ethiopia and Uganda, works we each imagined would be our magnum opus – but the problem, invariably, was how we might turn these into published books. Over beers and pizza, we brainstormed. Maybe what we needed was to be agented in London or in New York. Maybe what we needed was to move to London or New York. Maybe we simply needed to support each other, edit one another’s manuscripts. (But how would that help us get published?) Then, a Somali author who’d won some of Africa’s most important literary prizes for shorter work, chimed in. What we needed, he said, was to be adopted by Ellah Wakatama.

To be an African writer today – especially one writing in English, especially one younger than 40 – is to reckon with the idea of Ellah. Born in Zimbabwe when it was still under the Rhodesian flag, and now based in Britain, she is an editor-at-large at Canongate, an independent publishing firm. But it is more accurate to describe her as the most powerful woman – person – in Anglophone African literature today. From friends whose books she has touched, one hears of the fury of her red pen, of what they describe as the best editing they’ve ever experienced. From those who’ve worked with her, one hears of a person who doesn’t suffer fools, nor clichés like ‘suffer fools’. No other individual has so influenced the 21st-century African canon, if there is such a thing. Wakatama calls it ‘the bookshelf that my grandchildren will refer to’. Her lifelong project is to shape that bookshelf, to turn it into a library unto itself.

The night went on, as did the weekend. We’d all laughed when he said it, but beneath our laughter were three uneasy truths:

In the beginning was Chinua Achebe. It is a rule of African literature that any exegesis has to begin with him. Achebe is famous as a novelist – Things Fall Apart (1958) really is one of those great books that everyone should read before they die – but he was more important as an editor of Heinemann’s African Writers Series (AWS), which introduced Western readers to an array of African authors, fledgling and established alike. Novels by African writers had existed before Achebe but, for writers of a certain generation, the AWS was arguably the only publishing vehicle for their work. The great Ngũgĩ wa Thiong’o, for instance, had his first book published because he gave his manuscript to Achebe. Now we needed to give our manuscripts to Wakatama. She was editor, agent, publisher and godparent, all rolled into one.

But how had she become this person? How did a kid from Harare grow up to wield such influence? I, a kid from Kisumu, a city in Kenya, was determined to track how this had come to be.

The idea of Ellah is different from Ellah in person. In June 2025, I met Wakatama in Accra where she was leading a writing workshop for Pfumo, a Pan-African literary collective she’d recently co-founded. Who was this person in front of me, smoking a cigarette she’s rolled herself? So giggly, so cheery, a bit frilly. She squealed when we passed the store of her favourite Ghanaian designer. I wanted to gossip with her. She exuded fun.

I put to her my Achebe comparison, although I could already see holes: Wakatama’s hair is far more glorious and, unlike the grand old man of African letters, she’d never been a novelist, though she’d briefly tried to be a poet, and co-authored a book of short stories when she was 21. Wakatama, laughing, wouldn’t agree with the Achebe analogy – we both knew that would be in bad taste – but said she could see the throughline of my logic. She was, after all, a child of the age that the AWS represented and sought to shape: Africa after empire.

The second child of Winnie Ndoro and Pius Wakatama, Ellah spent her first four years in a village south of Harare with her grandparents. Winnie and Pius had met at work: she, educated, was a secretary; he, a township boy who sold newspapers, had, Ellah told me, very much ‘married up’. Pius didn’t finish high school but he could write in English; he’d read the short fiction in DRUM, a South African magazine he sold, and thought to himself: I can do that! He could, and did, and then became a novelist in the Shona language, well-known enough that he was invited for a residency at the University of Iowa.

‘I feel I have been looking for Narnia ever since. Every book I open, I’m looking for Narnia’

This time, the whole family went. In Iowa, little Ellah struggled to make friends, partly because she barely spoke English. Once, she saw a group of Black girls playing outside but they wouldn’t speak to her since all she spoke was Shona. That people who looked like her couldn’t understand her was deeply unsettling; soon after, Ellah stopped talking altogether for six months. When she resumed, she stuttered badly in English. Her mother, Winnie, enlisted a speech therapist who used C S Lewis’s The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe to help her. If you can read this word w-a-r-d-r-o-b-e without stuttering, the therapist once told her, we will have a peach melba. Ellah thought and thought, said the word without tripping over it, then snarfed down her reward.

Perhaps that’s where it started – the belief that books could be a salve against the world. A few years later, when her class went on a field trip to see a wardrobe that had once belonged to C S Lewis, Ellah packed spare socks, a sandwich and a flashlight because she was going to Narnia and not coming back. But when they opened it, it was just an empty wardrobe. ‘I was really disappointed,’ she told me. ‘And I feel I have been looking for Narnia ever since. Every book I open, I’m looking for Narnia.’

In 1976, the family moved back to Zimbabwe, where a war of liberation was being waged. A year before Ellah was born, the white minority in Rhodesia had seized power, declaring independence from Britain. Consequently, two liberation groups – ZANU and ZAPU – had started a resistance war, rejecting rule by a clique of white settlers. Pius and Winnie wanted to be home when it was won. Ten-year-old Ellah, no longer mute, had forgotten how to speak in Shona. Winnie enrolled her in the best private elementary school in Zimbabwe at the time. Only white girls were students at Bishopslea but Winnie saw no reason why her daughters shouldn’t get the best education. Neither did the school’s British headmistress. But the Rhodesian government was angry this had happened, and they deported the headmistress.

All the while, the war continued. Some of Ellah’s relatives were either fighting or living in villages fenced off because of the war. Her classmates were told to support their soldiers; one year, they had to knit balaclavas for the prime minister Ian Smith’s forces, so Ellah went home and asked her mother for green yarn. Winnie was furious; no daughter of hers was going to make balaclavas for the Rhodesians. Ellah cried and cried; all she wanted was to fit in with the other girls. As a compromise, Winnie bought her brown wool – nothing but the green of the Rhodesian flag would fit the Rhodesians.

‘It had a profound effect on me … this idea that your life has to have meaning, that you can’t just exist, that you have to be impacting the world’

This is one part of what shaped Wakatama. The memories of the war. The race laws in Zimbabwe. The fury with which her mother disregarded them while ensuring that her children were equipped to navigate them. The other part is books. Ellah moved from Nancy Drew and The Hardy Boys to Hermann Hesse and Taban Lo Liyong, working through her father’s library. She wrote Liyong a fan letter; to her delight, he responded. She and her best friend forged their mothers’ signatures so that they could borrow Georgette Heyer paperbacks from the adult library. Then, in 1980 – the year of the first free elections, of Zimbabwe’s independence, of the start of high school – she was assigned to read Doris Lessing in class.

Lessing’s The Grass Is Singing (1950) – the story of the relationship between Mary, a poor white farmer’s wife, and Moses, a Black servant on the homestead – was the first book Wakatama read that was set in Zimbabwe. She found it shocking – and confusing. ‘As a girl, I wanted to become a woman who stood at the vanguard of the battlelines that Lessing had set out, the questions she asked about selfhood and a woman’s place in the world,’ she would later say. ‘But Moses was the one who was my relative.’ It was a challenge in newly independent Zimbabwe to hold both those stories: ‘Lessing, on the one hand, speaking for my gender, and her boldly imagined Moses, on the other hand, bearing witness to my people’s dispossession.’

Enflamed by Lessing, she started writing fiction. A magazine called Moto had a monthly short-story competition, top prize $100, which she entered and won. Her story was about a prison inmate suffering an existential crisis – full of ‘deep emotion, injustice and pretty sentences’. With her prize money, she bought a pair of shoes, grey snakeskin. ‘My brother was at school with a Kenyan boy I really liked,’ she added. ‘So I took him and his friends out to lunch.’ When her father read the story, he said: ‘Yes, Miss Ellah, it’s very good.’ But he was a traditional writer; he believed stories should have a beginning, middle and end, and a moral lesson. Hers didn’t have any of that. ‘The next month, he sent in a story and he won,’ Wakatama told me. ‘It’s something a therapist would have a field day with but, in our house, it was a massive joke.’

Pius Wakatama was a man of strong opinions and believed all his children should have opinions, too. He would argue with them and expect them to argue back. ‘Are you a man or a mouse?’ he once asked Nhamu, Ellah’s younger brother, when he refused to engage. ‘Dad, I’m a mouse,’ Nhamu responded. (Ellah laughed at the memory.) Pius was also extraordinarily large-hearted, often bringing home people who were in a tight spot: struggling relatives, street families, hitchhikers. ‘I grew up with that kind of thing,’ Wakatama said. ‘I think it had a profound effect on me … this idea that your life has to have meaning, that you can’t just exist, that you have to be impacting the world.’ Once, at the school where he taught, he heard a student crying in the girls’ bathroom because her parents wanted to marry her off. ‘Don’t worry,’ Pius told the young woman. ‘Bring your suitcase with you tomorrow.’ He went home and told Winnie he was bringing her a new daughter. The next day, he knocked on Ellah’s bedroom door and said: ‘This is Florence, she’s your sister.’

Wakatama went to the same college as her mother, a small Mennonite school in rural Indiana. There, in her final year, she declared that she would not read. Specifically – and ‘with all the irritating self-riotousness of youth,’ as she once put it – she announced that she would not be reading any dead white males. Craft your own reading list, her professor told her. This was a turning point. ‘Even though it would be years later that I started working as a publisher, it defined how I approached publishing,’ she told me. ‘The bookshelf was wrong; I knew there was more to it. My professor challenged me to find that “more” – and I did.’

She put James Baldwin on her list, and Alice Walker. Then she discovered Tsitsi Dangarembga.

Dangarembga’s Nervous Conditions (1988) was the first book published in English by a Black Zimbabwean woman. It follows 13-year-old Tambu at a mission school and confronts the ‘nervous conditions’ – a phrase taken from Jean-Paul Sartre – of being caught between traditional African culture and Western colonial influence. The novel’s opening sentence – ‘I was not sorry when my brother died’ – jolted Wakatama, not least because Tambu’s brother had almost the same name as Wakatama’s brother, Nhamu. It felt as if, in exploring the dissonance of social mobility in Zimbabwe, Dangarembga was writing about Wakatama and her peers: she ‘felt that the author had peeked inside our most secret diaries and written us into being.’

More than a decade would pass before Wakatama found her way back to books. After college, she married Richard Allfrey, a British boy she’d met in the US, and, while in grad school, decided to have a child because she was ‘curious about motherhood’. She wrote her dissertation with little Gabrielle on her lap. (‘I’d breastfeed and type, breastfeed and type.’) Afterwards, the family moved to England, where she worked a series of jobs in the education sector. ‘I hated every single one,’ she said. The racism in England rankled her but, more than that, she was bored. One day, on holiday in Zimbabwe, her parents sat her down. Pius asked what she was doing with her life. Her mother added: ‘You’ve been sitting on your brain so long you’re going to forget to use it.’

‘They didn’t recognise me as a member of the tribe. I would hang out with the guys in the post room because they were Black’

Ellah cried after that intervention. She felt she was letting down her family, letting down Zimbabwe – in those early postcolonial years, the feeling was that your education was meant to improve things. Then her daughter unwittingly spearheaded another intervention. ‘We can’t live with you because you’re so unhappy,’ Gabrielle, seven at the time, told her after she returned from work one day. ‘You spend all your time and money on books,’ Richard chimed in. ‘Why don’t you just work in books?’

Gabrielle, now 34 years old, doesn’t remember giving her mother this advice, though she told me it must have been very good advice for a seven-year-old. She and her sister Rujeko, born 10 years after Gabrielle, were also in Accra. She was enjoying seeing her mother work with writers. Wakatama knew how to talk with writers in a way she hadn’t instinctively known how to talk to babies. When Gabrielle was born, Wakatama – unsure of how to interact with an infant – would read her the paper. This was the 1990s and the Soviet Union had crumbled, so she read Gabrielle stories about Boris Yeltsin. What Gabrielle remembers is struggling with dyslexia

and her mother encouraging her to read, being patient, open and supportive.

After that second intervention, Wakatama worked for a year as a temp at Penguin’s offices in London, before being hired as an assistant to both the editor of the Ancient Classics series and the editor of the Modern Classics series. She was 30. There were books everywhere, piles on the floor, spilling out of cabinets, teetering stacks on desks. I’m home, she thought to herself. But the trouble with being home is that sometimes not everyone there accepts that it is your home. ‘I knew I was with my tribe of people,’ she told me. ‘But they didn’t recognise me as a member of the tribe. I would hang out quite a bit with the guys in the post room because they were Black. Nobody was cruel to me – it just never occurred to them at the time.’

She paused. ‘I didn’t look like an editor because none of the editors looked like me.’

She’d sit at the desk looking at the Modern Classics: there were Russian writers, and Japanese writers, and French writers. She began to wonder where the African writers were. ‘How can you say you have a Modern Classics list, and you don’t have Achebe? You don’t have Ngũgĩ? It made absolutely no sense to me.’ Encouraged by her boss, she reached out to people who’d worked at the African Writers Series, and licensed some of those authors: Dambudzo Marechera from Zimbabwe, Bessie Head from Botswana, Ngũgĩ wa Thiong’o from Kenya. She became interested in Black writers from elsewhere, for instance championing the work of the Jamaican-born Linton Kwesi Johnson, who became the first Black poet, and the second living poet, to be included in the Penguin Modern Classics series.

‘I wasn’t at this point thinking I’m out to change literature,’ she told me. ‘I just thought this bookshelf is wrong. As a child of independence, you know you’re supposed to be changing the world, you don’t just submit and do your job. Plus, I was ambitious as fuck.’

It’s hard to write about editors. Who profiles editors? Writers are easier, at least published ones. Why did you say this 40 years ago, you might ask them; Why does the structure of your novel resemble Flaubert? While talking to Wakatama – we were discussing Wole Soyinka’s hair (‘like a cloud!’) and that time in 2010 she interviewed him – my own early obsessions returned to me: Peter Abrahams and the world of 1940s Johannesburg he created in Mine Boy (1946), Gustave Flaubert’s Parisian aristocrats in Sentimental Education (1869). I knew from an early age that I was going to be a writer; I’d been trapped by literature, deriving pleasure ever since from moments when I could do what those writers did: bring an idea in my head to life on the page, turn a half-formed thought or image into a sparkling sentence.

None of these moments would have been possible without an editor. Only a specific kind of obsessive understands Andy Elisburg’s role in the Miami Heat’s NBA championships, or Elvira Nabiullina’s in Vladimir Putin’s war on Ukraine, and it’s the same with editors. Writers know. Without an editor who ‘gets it’, we have little chance of succeeding – a sentence my editor most certainly did not force me to write. Or, think of musicians and their producers: there was no Michael Jackson without Berry Gordy, or Kendrick Lamar without Dr Dre. I mentioned Dre to Wakatama, specifically the way she stopped trying to be a poet and began publishing other writers, just as he killed his own rap career, and she cackled.

‘No one’s ever compared me to Dr Dre before!’

The books she is drawn to are ones ‘that feel like they had to be written’, she tells me later, when I ask about the bookshelf she is building, the one her grandchildren will refer to. ‘There has to be a message, a question, an exploration. This works best when filtered through character and place – so the story is equally important.’ In this, she has come to agree with Pius: a story needs a message and a plot. But she remains drawn to beauty. ‘It’s the layers underneath that will keep me reading and re-reading,’ she added. ‘I remain obsessed with beautiful sentences, that delicate crafting of the thought until it becomes poetry.’

Western readers should have to do the work that the African reader has always done

After Penguin, Wakatama moved to Jonathan Cape, an imprint of Random House. Among the first big writers whose books she worked on were Julian Barnes and Ian McEwan. She said: ‘I copyedited On Chesil Beach, which has one mistake in it.’

I said I would read the book to try to find it.

She laughed and said: ‘You won’t know the mistake. If you find it, tell me. Ian said it was his fault, not mine, but I still blame myself.’

Although she now had more room to commission books, her acquisitions budget was still tiny. She scoured the list of writers awarded grants by the Arts Council of England, reaching out to them to see if they had anything to publish. That’s how she published Biyi Bandele’s Burma Boy (2007), about African soldiers in the Second World War, and Nii Ayikwei Parkes’s debut novel Tail of the Blue Bird (2009), a thriller that blends forensic science and Ghanaian folklore. Both challenged the way she thought about editing. In a multi-lingual book, for instance, the non-English parts are usually italicised. But Ayikwei Parkes refused to submit to this; in Blue Bird, this convention is flipped. Typically, ‘English is the standard and the rest of us are deviations,’ she told me. But in Blue Bird, a lot of the narration is by a man who doesn’t speak English: only when he uses a non-Akan word (particular to English) do italics appear. Ayikwei Parkes’s insistence on italicising these individual words of English, rather than those of Twi or Ga, was an assertion that the centre of the world was where the writer said it was, that Western readers should have to do the work that the African reader has always done.

‘That was really, for me, political,’ she said.

Ayikwei Parkes told me how unexpected it had been to find an editor willing to change her mind about editorial style. ‘For most conventional editors, it might be baffling to accommodate such a conceit,’ he said. ‘But she fully grasped the political thrust of my stylistic choice and supported its use, protecting me from the clutches of standard copyeditors.’ When I mentioned the Achebe-Wakatama comparison to him, he disagreed. ‘I sort of resist the idea of godparents for African writing, a notion that doesn’t exist for Western writing. What I will say is that Ellah is, like Achebe, certainly interested in the dignity and autonomy of the African personality and perspective. She believes and understands that Africa is a continent from which the world can be observed, made sense of, influenced and remade.’

Growing up in Zimbabwe, Wakatama says, made her a better editor. ‘Because as an African, for my generation at least, you grow up in an education system that doesn’t include you in the literature; therefore, you have to fill in the gaps yourself. So almost any African, if they were a reader anyway, is going to be a better reader than an English person – because we’re always reading outside ourselves, as well as within ourselves.’ She paused. ‘Which I don’t think we acknowledge enough. I think, now that I’m older, especially post-menopause, and also senior enough not to care what anyone thinks, I can just say it out loud.’

It has taken decades of being dismissed for her to speak so freely: of working under bosses who paid lip-service to diversity, of going to award ceremonies and realising the only other Black people in the room were waiters, of having smaller purchasing and marketing budgets than her colleagues. She recalls trying to acquire a book once, championing it fiercely, but being turned down – mere months later, it was bought by another editor in a different division for five times the price. ‘I told them: This is nonsense – you could have had this book for five grand six months ago when I found it, but because someone else says that it’s good, you’re now going to take it. What the fuck?’

Above her desk at Random House, she’d put up a picture of Toni Morrison, previously an editor at the company. ‘I used to look at that picture and think: She survived and I can too.’ But it was after the death of Nhamu, her younger brother, that she decided things could not go on as they had. Nhamu had suffered a series of strokes, and his death a decade later catapulted Wakatama into a deep depression, a depression that would be matched in depth only by the one triggered by her later divorce from her husband. ‘[Nhamu] was one of the true loves of my life,’ she told me, dabbing her eyes. ‘I was a completely different person after he died … his dying made me think: If he’s gone and I’m left behind – if his life has ended, what am I gonna do with mine?’

‘My feeling is that, if I am sitting at that table, to not sit at that table for too long’

Shortly after, she joined Granta as deputy editor of the literary magazine. She loved its international scope and finally had what she’d always wanted: a proper budget for commissioning writers. She also began to commission authors for Granta Books. She said: ‘I am driven by a mission, I really am. I’m not a religious person, but I grew up in a religious family. Part of that is about what are you here on earth for? The most important thing to me is books. My daughters will absolutely tell you that they had to fight for my attention. And I want others to have the chance to experience what I’ve experienced. Granta finally gave me that opportunity to do that level of commissioning.’

She started doing workshops – in Nigeria, China, Sri Lanka – to train other editors, as well as more public talks and radio. Her first big interview was with Wole Soyinka. She read him a poem on stage; it was a poem he had written when younger about what he’d be like when his hair was silver. Ellah told him: ‘Look at you now, you’re pretty much all silver. You know, what do you think about the poem?’ The great man laughed.

In 2011, she was awarded an Order of the British Empire for her services to the publishing industry. Somebody noticed, she thought to herself, somebody noticed the work I’m doing. Meanwhile, some colleagues asked: Well, what are you getting that for? ‘We now call these microaggressions, which are never microaggressions,’ she said. ‘My answer was always, for surviving this.’

What does it mean to be the only person of your kind in a room, constantly having to prove you belong? For Wakatama, the cost was psychological. ‘I wish someone had said to me: This life that you’re living is really fulfilling your soul but beware of the damage it will do to you,’ she said. It is tempting to buy into the hoopla of being the first this-or-that, of having a seat at the table, ‘to get sucked into that sense of, Oh, I’m special,’ she warns. ‘My feeling is that, if I am sitting at that table, to not sit at that table for too long.’

Part of her mission, therefore, is to continue to facilitate the entry of gifted writers into the global publishing infrastructure. In this regard, the English writer Max Porter, who worked alongside Wakatama at Granta, describes her as ‘a proper writer’s editor, deeply committed to and connected to her writers, not necessarily just the one book she is working on.’ As someone new at the time to the publishing world and ‘a little suspicious of it already’, Porter was inspired by how Wakatama nurtured and protected the writers she worked with ‘in the line and on the page and in the world.’

In 2020, after the murder of George Floyd forced a new racial reckoning in the United States and beyond, Wakatama watched white agents rush to sign Black writers. ‘I was like, you’ve got to be kidding me,’ she recalled. ‘After 20 years of saying: Publish Black writers now, you have this.’ Young writers were suddenly being offered massive advances. ‘On one level, that’s great – I’m thinking: Baby, take the money and run! But when you pay half a million for a book and the book sells 10,000 copies, it’s a failure. Remember, it’s an advance against your royalties. So, the next time, you’re starting off in debt.’ The result is that the publisher thinks these books don’t sell and ‘you’ve made it impossible for those authors to be successful.’

In Accra, I sat in on some of the Pfumo sessions. One reason Wakatama co-founded the collective was her concern about how few African nonfiction writers were published internationally. As the participants discussed writing craft, she listened closely, her head tilted towards the speaker, her legs crossed. When she talked, she leaned back, her hands a vital part of her speech. Books surrounded us. (‘It’s overwhelming to realise how many of these I edited,’ she told me.)

One evening in 2017, Wakatama saw a post on Facebook by Dangarembga, the author of Nervous Conditions. That book’s opening sentence – ‘I was not sorry when my brother died’ – had shocked Ellah, but when her own Nhamu died, she returned to the novel in her sorrow. How astutely Dangarembga had written of what Ellah herself had to grapple with: ‘the psychic trauma of fitting into a world that seems to demand an erasure, a forgetting of the self before.’ Dangarembga’s second novel, The Book of Not, had come out in 2006. She was now seeking an editor for her third, which she would self-publish.

That Dangarembga would struggle to find a publisher despite her success was an indictment of an industry that still struggled to recognise African writers. Wakatama didn’t know Dangarembga personally but she reached out anyway. ‘Your writing is so important to so many of us, why are you self-publishing?’ she asked. ‘If you do self-publish, please can I offer my services for free as your editor?’

The new book – This Mournable Body (2018) – was remarkable, Wakatama thought, and she offered to introduce Dangarembga to a friend who ran Graywolf Press, an American indie publisher. Wakatama flew to Harare; Dangarembga came to the family house for lunch. When she was younger, Dangarembga recalled, she often corresponded with a Zimbabwean writer called Pius Wakatama. Was he a relative, Dangarembga wondered. Pius laughed. It was him.

Graywolf bought the book. Wakatama was then still editing it for free, but she added a clause in the contract stipulating that the publisher would pay her an extra (hefty) fee if the book made it to the Booker shortlist. ‘Everyone was fine with it because it was never going to happen,’ she recalled, smiling. But by now Wakatama knew to bet on herself. ‘When she got shortlisted and the money hit my account, I was, like, Yeah. I told you all.’
