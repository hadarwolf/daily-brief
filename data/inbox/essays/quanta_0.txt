Nothing really exists in isolation. Almost every part of our lives exists in a network. Consciousness arises from what neurons do together, life from what genes and proteins do together, society from what we do together. When researchers began mapping real-world networks in the 1990s – from the World Wide Web to Hollywood actors linked by films – they found that wildly different systems shared architectural features that their components alone could not explain. This suggested common organizing principles for how networks form and develop.

In this episode, two of the pioneers of network science – Albert-László Barabási and co-host Steven Strogatz – discuss how the field took shape, the ideas that underpin it, and why this is more than graph theory under a different name. The conversation then turns to health and nutrition. Disease-related genes cluster in “network neighborhoods”, Barabási explains, which may help identify better drug targets and predict which patients will respond to which drugs. That work led him to his Foodome project, which catalogs the molecules found in the food we eat and asks what they do inside our cells. By his count, around 135,000 compounds lie outside conventional nutrition, which concerns itself mainly with energy, building blocks, and vitamins. These compounds, which he calls the “dark matter of nutrition,” and their effects on the body are only beginning to be understood.

STROGATZ: Let me see if I got all that. It sounds super interesting. I’m led to assume from the way you’re describing it that rheumatoid arthritis could be triggered by a different disease module, and then naturally this drug would not necessarily help those patients.

BARABÁSI: A disease module is always the same, but you would have to perturb a different part of the module to treat you. Because the idea is that if this is your disease module, like a circle, and you’re on the left-hand side of the circle where your changes are, and Humira is hitting on the right-hand side, the impact of Humira will not actually travel all the way to you. So you need another drug that hits closer to you.

And this is not only for RA, right? For many diseases, we have multiple drugs, and doctors do a kind of a guess, guessing game or most likely scenario when they assign one drug or the other to the patient. And so this particular network medicine toolset points to a future where that guesswork is taken out.

STROGATZ: It’s nice. It’s becoming clear to me, and your map analogy really does help because, you know, just the map itself in the case of these biological unknown territories is- saves a lot of guesswork.

We often think about food that’s good for us or bad for us in terms of a few pretty coarse categories: fat, sugar, carbohydrates, protein, vitamins, all that. Can network thinking help us go beyond that kind of coarse view of what’s important in nutrition?

BARABÁSI: Of course, you wouldn’t ask the question if the answer would not be yes. But let me tell you how we got to that. It all came from the network medicine perspective because all the data that we use in network medicine comes from genomics. However, genomics alone cannot explain the degree that people get a disease.

So for example, when you look at the top ten reasons why people die in the United States, and you take them one by one from heart disease to cancers and other things, you find that only ten to twenty percent of the disease causation is explained by your genome. And then you always ask, “Where’s the rest?” Right? And they say, “Well, it’s in the environment.”

So about fifty years ago, we said, “Okay, well, what does this mean from a cellular perspective and a network perspective, that environment? And what are the environmental factors?” And of course, we know what the environmental factors are. There are exercise, there are sleep, there are stress. But there’s one gorilla in the room that is the biggest, which is food. Diet, right?

And so we said, “Okay, food is molecules, so why don’t we just map out somehow what does food do to our cells?” And the canonical picture is what food does is gives us energy, and nutrition is dealing with that, right? Because at the end, nutrition deals with the energy sources in the food from sugars to fats to carbohydrates and so on. And everything that you must have to survive, which is typically energy sources and some of the building blocks of the cells, as well as vitamins, are really well treated by nutrition.

So we started to map out what are the molecules that are known in the food industry to be in the food, and we were shocked to realize that there are many more than what nutrition tells us. Nutrition typically gives us about 150 components that are in the cell, and they are very well categorized. We know in every single food how much of that, what is the concentration and so on, whether it’s there or not. But then as we dig deeper, we realized first that there were more than 20,000 other molecules that are not nutritional components that were also in the food.

That was about five, six years ago. Now our databases have about 135,000 molecules that are not nutritional components that are carried by our food. So the question is: What are they? What do they do, right? And that kind of started the whole Foodome Project in the lab. On one end, the Foodome is focusing on mapping out what are the molecules that are known to be in the food, and the second one is that what do they do to our cells?

STROGATZ: Wow. This is like when people say I’m eating chemicals. Of course, everything’s a chemical, including the nutritious ones, but I think what they have in mind is all these — what did you say? A hundred thousand of them?

BARABÁSI: 135,000, our current database, right? And let’s understand what they are. So let me give you one example that I think illuminates a little bit the story: polyphenols.

We know that we’re supposed to eat lots of fruits and vegetables because they’re good for us, right? Why are they good for us? Because they have polyphenols and flavonoids, which are molecules that you don’t get in meat, or you don’t get in other sources, nor can we ourselves produce it. So, the first question is: why doesn’t meat have it? And why are they good for us? Always we get the answer, they’re antioxidant, they’re good for the cell.

But so, let’s first clean up why only plants have it? Well, the reason plants have it and animals don’t is because the plants don’t know how to run. Much of the animal difference is about muscles. So, we are optimized to create really, to use energy to create movement.

However, plants don’t know how to run, so their defense is totally chemicals. And to build those chemicals, they built a massive secondary metabolism. We have 20,000 genes. There are some flowers that have 150,000 genes, right? Because they need that massive amount of genes to really catalyze reactions that we don’t even dream about to produce these things, right? Even onion has 40,000 genes, twice as we do, right? So, either the onion is super intelligent doing things that we don’t know, or right, it’s just really producing that taste of the onion, which we cannot produce, but requires a massive secondary metabolism to do. So now, these polyphenols are good for us, but they are not nutritional components. They don’t provide energy to us. They’re invisible to our metabolism. Our metabolism does not see them. So, what do they do?

Well, when you eat a plant, it first is being encountered by the bacteria, which just break up things. But mostly bacteria are really eager to get sugar, so they don’t bother too much with the polyphenols. Ultimately, polyphenols, many of them get into your blood. Some of them are toxic. They’re being broken up a little bit in your liver, and then they will start going around in your cells.

And there, what they do is they start binding to your proteins and DNA. Their role is purely regulatory. They act like thousands of drugs that are micro-modulating the full activity of our cells. So they have a totally different role than all the nutritional components that we have.

Without them, we will start having lots of diseases in the long run. And one of the big challenge for all of us is to understand what polyphenols and the other 135,000 molecules do to us, how they modulate our health, and how could we use that knowledge to further improve health?

STROGATZ: Hmm. This is marvelous. I feel like I read somewhere while preparing for our conversation a phrase connected to something like dark matter or dark energy. What is your phrase, dark what?

BARABÁSI: Yeah, so when we published the first paper about the first 20,000 molecules, we called it the dark matter of nutrition because it’s not existing between the canon of nutrition. And I think many people simply thought that these are just inert molecules that you go in and goes out and doesn’t really affect us.

But when you look deeper, there’s lots of spread around literature about showing this polyphenol does this, that does that, this has anti-cancer, that has other properties, they clean up the cell from mess and so on. And so all these molecules that are non-nutritional components, which I said right now we’re about 135,000, we call it the dark matter of nutrition.

STROGATZ: So interesting. Are there some big open questions in this field of network science that, that you could reveal to us? What do you think are the great frontiers now?

BARABÁSI: It’s a good question, right? When I was a graduate student, I met one of the luminaries of the field, and I was like a bright-eyed graduate student, and I ask him, “You’re the big guy. What’s next?” and he said, “I don’t know, but I’m probably working on it.” And he was not.

So, grain of salt, you know. Often what’s next is really given by the next generation, right? They’re already working on it, and we don’t know that.

But I do have some ideas. I know where the big holes are in our story, right? One of them that we’re currently working on is a big opportunity opening up is the brain. Brain is a very special network. I call it a physical network because the links are no longer these handshakes that we talked about earlier or these kind of virtual interactions between the people, but the neurons are permanently connected by synapses in our brain. And that physicality matters because it’s a very dense environment, and you need to really understand the properties of these physical networks to really understand why the brain looks the way it does and ultimately how it functions. Physicality is a key part of that story, and that’s one area that I’m really excited about.

Also coming from that, before I came to networks, I was doing material science. And, I see a great opportunity to rethink what kind of networks could be materials that we don’t see now that could give rise to materials that are fundamentally different from anything that we have today. All the materials we have out there are either regular lattices, or randomly wired things.

How could we use this wealth of knowledge that we as a community have accumulated in the last 20-wise, 30 years, right? About how many complex networks look like and bring it back to the material space. And there’s a particular opportunity here because now we have 3D printing almost down to the molecular scale. So the question there is, if you could 3D print any networks you want, what network would you print?

BARABÁSI: So much fun to do this with you. Thank you so much.
