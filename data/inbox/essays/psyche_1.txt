For newcomers to Denmark, many things don’t always make sense. Parents leave sleeping babies outside cafés. Young children cycle to school alone. Primary schools don’t do homework, exams and competition. Success is quietly downplayed rather than celebrated. Meetings are spent revisiting the last meeting, planning the current one, eating together and preparing for the next meeting. Decisions are painfully slow, and everything is scheduled and regulated. Nothing is rushed. For me, and for many others who have made Denmark our home, much of everyday life initially seemed to defy the logic by which successful societies are supposed to function.

Marrying into Danish culture, and being a psychologist, I was intrigued by this, but had stopped worrying only when our eldest daughter reached junior high. Until then, I had to reluctantly trust the Danish line of reasoning that appears not only in schools, but across everyday life: you must first feel tryg.

What does it mean to feel tryg? The word is often translated as ‘safe’, but that is not quite right. It does not primarily refer to the absence of threat. Rather, it points to the presence of a particular state: the body at ease – calm, relaxed, with a sense of familiar warmth. Like the relaxed feeling of a child resting in her parents’ arms. In Danish, feeling tryg is not assumed to rise spontaneously. It is rather something that arises as a reaction to specific contexts that are deliberately cultivated. Members of the group implicitly take the responsibility to cultivate settings that promote and foster feeling tryg.

Certain conditions facilitate this emotional stance that allows us to be present, without scanning the environment or anticipating what might go wrong – or what might, suddenly, change. When feeling tryg, attention widens. The world can be met with a quiet, open curiosity. We take in what is happening around us and engage more easily and positively with others. We have the space to review the situation and be free to make the best decisions about what it requires of us.

In Danish culture, feeling tryg is not merely an inherent individual quality, it is rather a cultural and emotional concept that guides how the social world is designed. Predictable environments, well-known surroundings, consistent and trustworthy relationships, friendliness and pleasant spaces are all assumed to promote feeling tryghed – ‘safeness’ – for all.When tryghed is absent, the body becomes more alert and more prepared. We begin to scan for what might go wrong, and uncertainty is harder to tolerate. We know this from ordinary moments: entering a new group and trying to read the room, sitting in a meeting or a classroom with unclear expectations, or speaking with someone whose tone is harsh or reactions unpredictable. Attention narrows, shifting away from what is being said and toward anticipating what might go wrong. On a basic level, the questions that preoccupy the mind are: friend or foe? Should I engage? Can I leave? Should I protect myself?

Danes extend tryghed everywhere: in classrooms, workplaces and public spaces

In line with decades of psychological research, the neuroscientist Stephen Porges has recently described how states of perceived safeness – a felt sense of being at ease – allow the nervous system to settle, making openness, joyful learning and social engagement possible. In this state of tryghed, tolerance and curiosity flourish, and so does prosocial behaviour. Unlike in traditional psychology, in Denmark, tryghed is not treated as a private matter. Creating environments that are tryg is seen as a shared responsibility and something that Danes take very seriously. This means that attention shifts from how individuals feel to how we structure shared conditions – and to how everyone feels. Physical and social settings are organised around this principle. Danes place a strong emphasis on cultivating environments that convey expectations very clearly – preferably in a friendly way. Actively fostering tryghed helps life feel pleasant and predictable enough for everyone to drop their guard. This is not just ‘safety’ in a physical sense, it’s a set of conditions that allows feelings of safeness to emerge in the group.

In Danish, these conditions are often referred to as ‘rammer’ or the ‘frameworks’ – in which tryghed for everyone is cultivated. Danes value tryghed and seek to extend it everywhere: in classrooms, workplaces and public spaces.

Certain rammer – certain conditions – allow tryghed to emerge. Over time, repeated experiences of feeling tryg accumulate into a form of implicit social knowledge: how to design the very conditions that make tryghed possible.Cycling to work one morning, I passed an election poster. In large letters it read: Trygge mennesker tør – ‘People who feel tryg dare.’ The message was simple, almost self-evident. When we feel this deep safeness, we are free to engage, to innovate, to act, to move forward. It was not presented as a psychological insight, but as something one could assume people already understood – an election appeal to shared values and what does not need to be justified.

Once you start understanding tryghed in Denmark, it appears everywhere. In how classrooms are organised, in how school curricula are set, in how meetings are run, in how public spaces are designed. The same quiet premise: if people feel tryg, something else becomes possible.

Meetings and schedules are structured to reduce unnecessary uncertainty

In schools, the emphasis is not only on what children learn, but on designing physical and social environments in which they can learn. In family life, there is often a focus on maintaining pleasant and predictable structures and schedules. In neighbourhoods with many children, the streets are empty at 6 pm – everyone is at home having dinner.

In workplaces, meetings and schedules are structured to make expectations explicit and to reduce unnecessary uncertainty. Time is taken to align, to clarify, so that people know what is required of them. In public life, this orientation appears in small, taken-for-granted behaviours: people tend to follow shared rules, and actively maintain a sense of order that others can rely on. Dog poop is picked up, traffic rules are followed, common spaces are designed for safeness, and in turn are highly respected. Individuals intentionally maintain the conditions that foster safeness for everyone every day – and everything that ‘safeness for all’ brings with it. This commitment extends well beyond everyday interactions. It is reflected in the entitlements of society, from paid parental leave for both mothers and fathers to free healthcare and education, all designed to reduce unnecessary uncertainty across the life course.

None of this is absolute, it is not always consistent, and this commitment brings its tensions. For Danes, what is unfamiliar, unpredictable or new can easily be felt as unsettling. Decisions can be painfully lengthy, as more perspectives are considered and alignment is sought. Processes become careful, sometimes to the point of hesitation. Groups move toward consensus – because shared understanding is given high priority. It also affects how difference is experienced: ways of thinking or behaving that fall outside established patterns may feel less tryg, simply because they are unfamiliar. In this sense, tryghed does not only enable openness; it can also limit what is accepted or allowed to emerge.

Rules are designed to allow everyone to feel safeness – but they can make processes slower and heavier. The same conditions that create stability can make quick change more difficult. Over time, tolerance for messiness, ambiguity and rapid adjustment may be less practised, which can, in turn, affect resilience. Can a country that relies so heavily on tryghed be competitive in a rapidly changing world?

Stable relationships and trustworthy frameworks form an infrastructure that supports prosocial behaviour

As a tryg-driven society, Denmark is often described as stable and high-trust, yet it also performs remarkably well across measures such as innovation and economic performance. While liberal democracies are in decline, Denmark also consistently ranks high on the democracy index. Democracies are many things, but they depend heavily on systems of emotional regulation: when institutions and norms foster predictability and shared responsibility, trust and cooperation emerge; when they erode, suspicion and hostility take over.

One way to make sense of this is to shift the focus to everyday life. Stable relationships and trustworthy frameworks – designed to benefit all – form an infrastructure that supports prosocial behaviour. They paradoxically make learning and innovation less risky in an uncertain world.

Often in liberal democracies, political life is framed around concepts like ‘effectiveness’, ‘freedom’ or ‘equality’. In Denmark, the emphasis is more often placed on the felt experience of tryghed – and on whether everyday life promotes feeling safeness – through everyday acts of care performed by individuals and people in office alike. When safeness is not cultivated, a different logic takes hold: under conditions of uncertainty and threat, exclusion can begin to feel rational, and control preferable to openness and trust.

Last spring, I was standing with some of the ‘international’ parents on the grass, watching the annual school football match. These parents – like myself – had settled into life in Denmark, despite having other, sometimes more lucrative, options in the United States, the United Kingdom, Germany or Canada. Among these parents there are CEOs of startup companies, heads of venture capital firms and high-profile consultants. We stood on the sidelines, watching our children run across the field, as one of the parents, Jeff, described something that had become difficult to explain to friends back home: how easy everyday life here felt.

Children cycling alone to school. Wallets returned. Streets kept clean. Systems that work. But also, something less visible – the sense that these conditions are not accidental. That someone, somewhere, has thought about how life should feel, and that this is a shared endeavour.

It is easy to notice the outcomes. It is harder to see the orientation behind them. Not only a commitment to rules or institutions, but to the ongoing work of maintaining conditions in which everyone can let their guard down. Perhaps this is what it means, in practice, to organise a society around care and tryghed – and why it may matter more than we tend to notice.
