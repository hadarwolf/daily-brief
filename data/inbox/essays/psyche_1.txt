While I’ve come across various literary and artistic works praising the art of looking, what truly led me down this path was Nirmal Verma’s Ek Chithda Sukh (1979), translated as A Rag Called Happiness (1993). In this Hindi novel, the 14-year-old narrator Munnu writes moving descriptions of everyday life in his notebook: ‘This I’ll remember, the boy told himself; I’ll put it down in my diary. Bitty was hanging clothes out to dry. A pale light filled the room. Darry sat back against the wall, his eyes on Bitty …’

Under its influence, I began to narrativise the world around me, so that my journal entries became ready reckoners for the future me: I’d simply page through for what was happening in my life outside the usual things we remember – family, friends, work, city living.

I catalogued the ephemera that tend to slip between the days and the headlines. The dreams I had and probably hated; my thoughts about a bitter cold in the middle of summer; my anxiety before a job interview; the excitement of watching a movie that overwhelmed me.

These are details we tend to gloss over in a 24-hour cycle, but in aggregate they accrue meaning – they slow me down, make me more observant. They denote a sense of time and the self, coming together in a way their digital counterparts don’t. It helps that I journal with pen or pencil, not phone or app, the better to etch the small things in my mind.

At the end of A Rag, all Munnu has are these recollections of times spent with Bitty and her gang of theatre friends over the summer. The break, the year, all these people, pass him by so simply, but the filled-up pages of his notebook, all the observations he has amassed, will stay forever as markings of a time lived – well or not, only time can tell.

I will confess that, when it comes to sports, I am a fair-weather fan. I like basketball but had little interest in the New York Knicks until they made it to the NBA finals in June. I’m barely interested in soccer unless the Canadian team or the Korean team make it at least to the World Cup round of 32. I root for teams when there’s cause to hope and, when they do advance, I’m as joyful and riotous as any fan.

My intermittent fandom used to be a source of sheepishness. It’s easy to feel, or be made to feel, like a fake fan or a poseur. Especially if you’re among ‘real’, lifelong fans. But this year, I’ve learned to embrace fair-weather fandom as a legitimate way to join in with a collective experience. While there will always be someone out there who might grumble about my fan status or try to flex their superior knowledge of player history, I’ve found that most people are just happy you’re celebrating with them – that you’ve come to recognise a team’s greatness.

Jumping on bandwagons is a normal and valuable part of life, really. We all use cues from the people around us to decide when and where to shift our attention. And if, for a brief window of time, we turn that gaze to a sports team we wouldn’t ordinarily focus on, that’s our prerogative. After all, watching sports can be really good for our psychological wellbeing – in part, research suggests, because of the social connection it promotes as we watch along with other fans. I particularly love those moments when a crowd of like-minded fans grows tense, hearts all racing together, until our team pulls through and we erupt into roars. When there’s an opportunity to participate in that shared drama and excitement, we should seize it.

Last month, while watching Christopher Nolan’s new epic The Odyssey, I couldn’t help but marvel at the patience of Penelope, who waits 20 years for her husband – the king, Odysseus – to return to Ithaca. Despite no indication of when, or if, he might return, she is steadfast in her wait.

I cannot claim such patience. My husband has never left me to manage a kingdom, but I’ve been plagued by plenty of frustrating waits. I am as impatient about small delays, like queueing at the post office, as I am about the longer, life-altering ones. I remember how exasperated I felt by the multiple delays in moving to our first home, not to mention my impatience now at waiting nine years for an endometriosis diagnosis.

I’ve long assumed that impatience is simply part of my personality. But I recently came across a different perspective: according to the psychologist Kate Sweeny and her colleagues at the University of California, Riverside, impatience is not a fixed facet of who we are. Instead, it’s an emotion – and patience is the process by which we manage it. While this isn’t a novel theory, Sweeny’s team tested it recently with promising results – they found, for instance, that it’s not so much the length of the wait that matters, but how fair we consider it to be.

I find the implications hopeful. Just as we can learn to handle other unpleasant emotions, we can all develop better skills and techniques to deal with impatience.

As with other difficult emotions, one key to regulating impatience is likely to be acknowledging it in the first place. We see this in Penelope: she allows herself to feel the impatience, but finds a way through it. The next time I’m faced with a long delay, I’ll channel my inner Greek queen and know that it’s an emotion I can manage, even if I can’t control the wait itself.

Mindfulness experts tell us to be present and experience whatever is in front of us. Take a raisin, observe its wrinkles, smell it, taste it carefully in a conscious mode. This is supposed to be an antidote to the absence of presence so common in our age of digital distraction.

As an occasional exercise, I’m sure it’s fine; but I’m sceptical about it as a fundamental way of being. Humans evolved with selective conscious attention, ignoring noise, responding preconsciously to environments, projecting expectations onto them. We ignore the present and we go beyond the present. That serves us well. Imagine taking a ride in a taxi driven by a mindfulness enthusiast who is relishing the light glinting from the bumper of the car in front, the particular smell of the vanilla air freshener, refusing to think ahead…

If you really want to practise mindfulness despite the risk of becoming like that taxi driver, try this antidote: meditate on absence.

In a passage near the beginning of Being and Nothingness (1943), Jean-Paul Sartre describes arriving late to meet his friend Pierre in a café. He looks around: there are people, tables, cigarette smoke, sounds, smells, colours, a ‘plenitude of being’. ‘I was expecting to see Pierre,’ he writes, ‘and my expectation has made Pierre’s absence happen as a real event.’ As he puts it, ‘Pierre absent haunts this café’.

Here’s the meditation. Sit quietly for 10 minutes. Focus on someone you care about and who you expected to be with you now, but isn’t. Let their absence haunt you. Recognise that the presence of this specific felt absence is an important aspect of who you are that colours so much. This is as much a part of your experience of the world as the wrinkles on a raisin. Acknowledge and relish that, then return to your mostly preconscious way of being.

Growing up, I always admired my grandmother’s relationship with plants. Every spare inch of her small backyard was used to grow vegetables and herbs for her kitchen, including medicinal Asian plants like mugwort. She would make teas, wines and jams from berries she’d forage along trails in Vancouver.

On visits to her house, I seldom spent time with her in the backyard, and joined her on foraging walks only once or twice. I never learned to tell which berries are edible, how to find promising spots for delicious treasures, or even how to tend a garden.

Thinking about the wisdom slipping through my fingers, I’m reminded of a Japanese ecologist I admire, Masashi Soga. Soga studies what he calls the ‘extinction of experience’: a phenomenon where people’s enthusiasm for and access to experiences that involve nature – such as gardening, hiking, birdwatching or other nature activities – wane with each successive generation.

Preserving these ties to the natural world is important, Soga argues, for several reasons. People who spend more time outdoors and feel more connected to nature often see benefits to their physical and mental health, and they are much more likely to care about climate change and conservation. Should the extinction of experience continue without intervention, we risk permanently losing those connections, both because of literal extinctions and because of our solidified disinterest.

In an effort to break the cycle for myself, I recently took a foraging class to learn how to identify and harvest plants like mugwort, out in public spaces. I learned about best practices for finding plants that are safe to eat, the etiquette for harvesting invasive versus native plants, and how to tell apart certain plants from their lookalikes. While this one-off class is not the whole answer, I hope it’s one of many steps to de-extinction.
