Chatter floats around me and smoke rises from the bonfire. It is a warm August night in the Philadelphia suburbs, and one of my friends slowly taps the darbuka under his arm. Then he sings Umm Kulthum’s ‘Alf Leila Wa Leila’ (‘A Thousand and One Nights’). One after another, we join in, singing along to what I once considered the epitome of old people’s music:

The next line comes to me instinctively. I’m singing in ecstasy, as my parents used to on our night rides. I laugh to myself as I watch my friends sway in their seats.

I’m back at my grandparents’ house in the Palestinian town of Beit Sahour. After playing in their yard until sunset, my cousins and I would gather on their bed, where Teta would turn down Umm Kulthum on the television to tell us the story of the Elderly Woman and her Cow while slicing apples. Now and then, I’d glance at the flickering black-and-white screen to see the audience clap, sway and cry out in a state of tarab, surrendering to what they called ‘her grandeur’. She would finish a stanza, and the audience would erupt in applause, calling out Azama ala azama, ya Sit! (‘Greatness upon greatness, oh lady!’), waiting for her to sing again.

They were out of their minds, I thought. Why such reverence for an Egyptian woman with dark sunglasses, a silk handkerchief draped over her hand, standing on a stage for hours accompanied by a full orchestra, repeating almost the same lyrics relentlessly?

Morning came and so did Fairuz. I woke up to my grandfather’s voice calling me. Eyes half-open, I put on my school uniform and ran down the stairs to my grandparents’ house, the scent of Arabic coffee rising from the stove. My Teta was in the backyard. She embraced me with her flour-dusted hands, then continued kneading the dough, humming along with the voice on the radio:

Fairuz, again. I expressed my dismay at the music choice, only to hear Teta’s monologue on the renowned Lebanese vocalist, the great ‘Bird of the East’, and the horrific possibility of a morning without her songs. I sighed and accepted my fate. I rolled my eyes and tapped my foot, taking a bite of Teta’s bread and waiting to be saved by my ride to school.

Naively, I had forgotten that my suffering would not end. It felt like an epidemic when Fairuz’s voice trailed from Teta’s radio to Amo Essam’s van. Oblivious to my frustration, with a cigarette between his teeth, he would sing along:

We left Beit Sahour when I was 15 for a ‘better life’, my father said – one where his kids’ futures would not be restricted by political turmoil and economic instability, but filled with security, opportunity and dignity that presumably awaited us in the West. So, in the summer of 2017, we packed our bags, gathering whatever crumbs of our life in Beit Sahour we could carry, and headed for the United States.

Philadelphia was beautiful – tall buildings and ancient architecture, enormous roads, and cheesesteak shops around every other corner. As we explored the suburbs for a place to settle, we drove past rows of identical houses with gable roofs, vast stretches of lawn and fast-food chains. Walmart was a sensational experience. It was our family’s picnic destination for the first few weekends in the US. I couldn’t believe there was a store with gardening tools, clothes, groceries and toys, all under one roof. There seemed to be infinite choices for everything – milk, bread and even eggs: cage-free, organic, conventional, and free-range. I had no clue what any of it meant. It was just eggs.

The mornings were quiet. I started eating a blueberry muffin on my way to school. I sat alone on the bus, where the speakers blasted country music. I had nothing to say to the bus driver. I searched for an English equivalent of Ya’tik Al Afiye. But my tongue was tied, and I settled for ‘Thank you.’

My ears searched for Arabic across campus. Before long, I was surrounded by people who looked and spoke like me

Amid the white American student body, my frizzy hair, bushy brows and distinctive nose made me conspicuous. Although students invited me to join their table, I sometimes chose to eat lunch alone, especially when I had a za’tar mankoushe – flatbread with dried crushed thyme and olive oil. I dreaded explaining that, no, I was not having marijuana for lunch.

I was not necessarily left out – kids liked to sit with me and ask me about my life in Palestine – but I always felt like an exotic creature among them. I couldn’t follow their conversations and cultural references. Listening to Taylor Swift, watching American TV or wearing flannels with miniskirts never quite made me feel that I belonged. Back with my family, I would exhale a long breath of Arabic phrases, exhausted from a seven-hour shift of imitating an American accent.

In college, I gravitated toward Arabs wherever I found them – and I was determined to find them. My ears searched for Arabic across campus, and when I detected it, I was filled with such giddiness that I ran to introduce myself. Before long, I was surrounded by people who looked and spoke like me. For the first time in my life, my closest friends were Jordanian, Lebanese, Syrian and Egyptian. We ate za’tar mankoushe together, argued over who made the best shawarma, laughed at the differences between our accents and traded stories about our homelands.

In each conversation, we searched for fragments of our old lives we still longed for: tea with our grandparents, soccer in the streets with the neighbours, the small corner stores in our neighbourhoods that sold cigarette-shaped candy. We remembered our elders’ obsession with listening to Fairuz in the morning and Umm Kulthum at night – and our irritation whenever they did.

I lost Teta and Sedo a few years later. I watched my father mourn both of his parents as he said goodbye to them over video calls, and we livestreamed their funerals on Facebook. Overcome with grief, I played Fairuz:

I listened to the songs that my Teta played every morning, the ones I couldn’t wait to escape.

Fairuz’s voice carried me back to my grandparents’ backyard, where Teta hummed along to the radio as her hands kneaded the dough. Lemons dangled from the tree above her. Coffee brewed on the stove as she handed me a cup of mint tea and a piece of freshly baked bread. Then Sedo would greet me with my nickname: Ahlan wa Sahlan, Alooseh, habibet Sedo.

I had become the person I used to roll my eyes at. In the diaspora, I found my way back to Umm Kulthum and Fairuz. What once belonged to my elders had quietly become my own.

As a little girl I used to fall asleep on chairs at weddings, only to open my eyes to find my parents and grandparents, my uncles and their wives, holding hands and leaning into each other singing along to Umm Kulthum’s ‘Alf Leila Wa Leila’, more enchanted with every stanza – and perhaps a little more in love.

Now, with my fair share of love, longing and heartbreak over men, I find my yearning reflected in Umm Kulthum’s songs.

At my friend’s engagement last winter, three generations sang along to Umm Kulthum. With eyes closed and glasses raised, hands swaying and some crying out ‘Allah’, we were in a state of tarab.

Back at the bonfire, I look around at my friends. Some had left home at a young age; others know it only through their parents. We are singing the songs we once thought belonged to our grandparents:
