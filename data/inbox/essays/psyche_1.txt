One dark night in 1683, a 19-year-old girl from the small town of Saint-Paul-Trois-Châteaux, not far from Grenoble in southeastern France, approached the fire in the hearth of her family home. This was a time of high religious tension in France, and Blanche Gamond’s Protestant family had been forced to host soldiers in their house whose goal it was to intimidate them into converting to Catholicism. They were not alone in being subjected to this treatment, known as the dragonnades policy, which had been instigated by Louis XIV’s government two years earlier. Over the course of the Sun King’s reign, anti-Protestant measures continually increased. These included restrictions on when and how Protestants could hold funerals, and prohibitions against Protestant women working as midwives; conversions to the religion were also forbidden.

Gamond had heard of Antoine Chamier, a man in the nearby town of Montélimar, being broken on the wheel for his refusal to renounce his religious beliefs. She admired Chamier’s steadfastness, and wanted to prove to herself that she, too, could endure great suffering for her faith. In approaching the fire, her aim was to test herself, and to prove that she was strong enough to withstand the pain. But Gamond could not do this. ‘As soon as my fingers began to burn,’ she wrote (my translation), ‘I would immediately withdraw my hand.’ This was no casual test of strength, however, and Gamond was devastated by her inability to tolerate the heat.

Though many people today don’t associate exile with western Europeans, the experiences of French Protestants in the early modern period were often fraught with uncertainty and suffering. They departed their homeland in search of security and stability elsewhere, and they often established large communities in the lands in which they settled, with churches catering to their diaspora. When leaving France, they often brought skills in fields such as silk-weaving or watchmaking with them, resulting in what some commentators have labelled as a ‘brain drain’ in their home kingdom.

So, while it may be difficult for those of us who have grown up in stable or safe countries without persecution, and who are part of a majority, to comprehend Gamond’s conviction and the danger with which it was associated, these were very common experiences for many people in history. Today, as well as in the past, humans tend to want to see ourselves as part of something bigger than us, a grand cause, something that will survive us, a religion or a nation or a cause – and many have sacrificed for the values or ideals they believe it represents.

As for how it occurred to Gamond to briefly burn her hand, it seems likely that she was inspired by the story of Thomas Bilney, a 16th-century Englishman who tested himself in much the same way the night before he was to be burnt at the stake. Though Bilney also initially instinctively retracted his hand when the flame tickled his fingers, the second time he attempted this feat, he succeeded in withstanding the heat for a couple of seconds. Bilney’s story featured in a popular martyrology (a collection of martyr stories) owned by many Protestant families in 17th-century France. The tales of those who remained firm in their faith against all odds – even in the face of torture or death – were designed to be inspiring, to provide comfort to readers through examples of those who had calmly met their end.

But these stories could also be sources of pressure, as we can see in Gamond’s account. Frustrated that she couldn’t properly emulate the martyrs of old, she frequently pleaded with God to make her better, more pious, less attached to the world and to herself: ‘My God, make it so that I love neither the world, nor the things in it, give me the grace … to renounce myself.’ This desire to be detached from worldly concerns was a common theme in medieval and early modern Christianity, and even in the earlier traditions of classical antiquity: it is often known as contemptus mundi, or ‘contempt for the world’. In making these appeals to God, Gamond was drawing on language that she had likely picked up from pastors and religious services, employing it in order to try to cope with a period of stress and uncertainty.

In 1685, rights previously granted to French Protestants – or Huguenots, as they were also known – were stripped back through the revocation of the Edict of Nantes, an event often simply called the Revocation. Persecution of this group grew so intense that many sought to leave their homeland. Historians estimate that around 200,000 Huguenots left France in the wake of the oppression. This exodus became known as the Refuge, and it was, in fact, as a result of this upheaval that the word ‘refugee’ entered the English language – from the French ‘réfugié’.

Of course, there was nothing new in history about populations being uprooted – the Huguenots, themselves, took inspiration from the story of the Babylonian exile of the Jews in the Bible. And, indeed, previous generations of French Protestants had also settled far from France, in bids to escape the Wars of Religion (1562-98). But the post-Revocation Huguenots were perhaps among the first groups of people to seek shelter in foreign climes after the Thirty Years’ War (1618-48) had helped plant the seeds for the development of the modern nation-state. The idea that a state might be intrinsically associated with a particular ethnic group living within its borders would remain in flux for many years before further solidifying amid the Romantic nationalism of the 19th century – however, the 1648 Peace of Westphalia was among the factors that helped lay the groundwork for this idea to take hold, through its assertion of the principle of territorial sovereignty.

Gamond sought to steel herself against physical pain, whereas Cabrit removed himself from harm’s way

Huguenots have furthermore been labelled ‘modern’ refugees by scholars such as John Hintermaier due to their propensity to appeal to the altruistic sentiments and economic concerns of those in their host nations, rather than seeking support based on religious affinity alone. The achievements of many Huguenots and their descendants in the textile industries and trade networks of their host nations have led to the community garnering praise as an example of successful integration. However, the path to a stable and secure future in a new land did not always run smooth. Another Huguenot, the minister Jacques Cabrit, initially moved to London, but wrote that people there ‘recognised from our mannerisms and our clothes that we were French, and we were subject to verbal and sometimes physical abuse’ (my translation).

Cabrit and his friends were, nonetheless, prepared to settle in England, until they heard rumours that King James II was planning to persecute Protestants in much the same way as Louis XIV had done in France. The threat of such an attack against their religion proved too much to bear, and on 1 March 1688, just a few months before the English king was overthrown, Cabrit set off again, eventually settling in Prussia. It may seem, then, that religious matters were more central to Cabrit’s identity than national affiliation, if it was those rumours that proved to be the last straw for him rather than actual instances of xenophobia.

But we can reflect on the role that fear can play in already tense environments. Cabrit, by leaving England, and Gamond, by trying to burn herself, both acted in the ways they felt would best protect them, in the face of an uncertain future. In this context, Gamond sought to steel herself against physical pain, whereas Cabrit removed himself from harm’s way.

Cabrit’s account also provides us with a reflection on how cultural differences can affect the expression of emotions. While employed as a French tutor in the home of an aged professor in Frankfurt an der Oder in Prussia, Cabrit witnessed the death of the elderly man. Though Cabrit was taken aback by this reaction, he did write that he admired the woman’s ‘stoicism’. This was just one of the clashes between cultural expectations and reality that he was to experience while adapting to life in German-speaking lands. We know that different cultural traditions value emotions, and their expression, differently, as scholars such as Barbara H Rosenwein have shown. It would appear that, in the Languedoc context – at least, according to Cabrit – women were expected to be highly vocal, and even clamorous, in the expression of their grief. It may have been that these raw evocations of pain were viewed as more authentic, signs of a close bond with the deceased. The Frankfurt an der Oder widow’s response, on the other hand, suggests a cultural context where measured reactions were valued, even amid difficult circumstances.

Eventually, Cabrit succeeded in settling down and living a relatively normal life in Cottbus, near Potsdam, in what is now eastern Germany. The same cannot be said for Gamond, who died in Zurich aged just 53. Imprisoned following an initial attempt to escape from France, she eventually managed to cross the border into Switzerland in 1688. Records show that she and her father requested alms in order to survive, and it is thought that the tragic and extremely pious image she painted of herself in her memoir was played up so as to attract sympathy and, thus, monetary assistance. And yet, there is also raw emotion to be seen in her words. While describing a deeply painful operation on her leg, Gamond wrote that she could not bring herself to give further detail: ‘[My quill] falls from my hand, and this sad memory makes me shed so many tears that I could [not] continue anymore [to endure] the horrible pains that I felt.’ The page on which these words are written in the manuscript is blotchy, in a way that might lead us to speculate over the presence of centuries-old tear stains. Whether or not tears are indeed the cause of the smeared writing cannot be determined, but one thing is certain: even after establishing oneself in exile, past pains and anxieties could continue to echo in early modern refugee lives.

While Cabrit and Gamond each ultimately led very different lives, both of their experiences were profoundly shaped by the persecution that their community faced. Both sought to carve out spaces for themselves within their religious and social community following their departure from their homeland, Gamond through writing and circulating an autobiographical account of her experiences, Cabrit mostly through pastoral work. Both leaned heavily on their networks for support, contacting family members, friends of friends and distant relatives in times of need. This experience of reaching out to others was shared by many other members of the Huguenot diaspora, and we can see from their stories that it was often thanks to being able to build on existing connections that refugees were able to thrive in new lands – though this was not always easy or even possible, as Gamond’s challenging experience in the Swiss cantons shows. While popular narratives about Huguenot trajectories often focus on the community’s economic and social success following their settling in new lands, we should not overlook the pain associated with being uprooted from one’s home that was experienced by many of these refugees. What they managed to achieve in spite of these difficulties is a testament to their resilience.
