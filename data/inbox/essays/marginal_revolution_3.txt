The author is my colleague Vincent Geloso and the subtitle is A New History of American Growth and Inequality, 1865-1945. Here is an excerpt from the very beginning:

…regardless of what improvements you think occurred during the Gilded Age and the Progressive Era, you are underestimating how much improvement there truly was. From 1870 to the start off World War I, America experienced the fastest and most stunning rise in the quality of life of the downtrodden, the poor, the unskilled, the midskilled, and the average Joe (i.e., the poorest 90% of Americans) ever seen in human history up to that point. Second, it was the period from World War I to the end of World War II that was marked by slower — though still substantial — gains for people at the bottom of the income ladder. Third, the improvements in the living standards of the poorest 90 percent of the population were faster than those of the top 10 percent and as fast as those of the top 1 percent from 1870 to 191. During those four decades, America was a lean and mean egalitarian growth machine. Fourth, this machine was fueled by America’s exceptionally dynamic market economy, which allowed people to move to pursue better opportunities (either in terms of industries or in terms of geography). While some state actions did contribute to this machine, market forces own the lion’s share, and, in many cases, they actually made government actions more effective. Fifth, America developed a welfare state only because, in the aftermath of World War I, the growth machine slowed down.

An excellent and much-needed book, definitely recommended.
